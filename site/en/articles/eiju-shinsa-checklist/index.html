Life Information · YU · 2026.11.21

The permanent residence review process has a new calculation method. Make sure to check these items before submitting your application.

Income is now compared to the average of a Japanese household of the same size. Pension estimates and B1-level Japanese proficiency are also being factored in. Depending on when you submit your application, different rules may apply.

The permanent residence guidelines were revised on October 1, 2026, and the changes are even more detailed than the news headlines suggest. Many people get stuck on items that were never asked about before; how income is calculated and how far back pension records are checked are now all clearly laid out. 🎫

The way income is calculated has changed. The guidelines look at the annual income of the entire household, which must consistently exceed the average income of a Japanese household of the same size. Only those living together and sharing finances are counted; however, adult family members who are no longer dependents are excluded. Income earned by a spouse on a Dependent visa through 'Designated Activities' is not included in the household annual income. Overseas dependents are still counted toward the household size, and for households of 5 or more, an additional living expense allowance is applied. These are factors for consideration, not a guaranteed threshold for approval; all circumstances are evaluated as a whole. The guidelines do not publish a fixed amount, so do not treat figures circulating online as a hard threshold. Comparing your own tax certificate with the average for a household of your size is a more accurate approach.

The timeline is also important. In principle, the new guidelines apply fully to applications submitted on or after April 1, 2027. Two items—income and the 'will not become a public burden' requirement—will retroactively apply to applications submitted on or after April 1, 2026, that were still under review as of October 1, 2026. Even if you have already submitted your application, you might be affected by the new calculation method.

For those with a 3-year period of stay, there are transitional arrangements regarding the 'holding the longest period of stay' requirement for permanent residency. If you apply by March 31, 2027, a 3-year period is still considered the longest. For those applying on or after April 1, 2027, you must meet three conditions to qualify: you must have held a 3-year status as of March 31, 2027, this must be your first permanent residency application, and the decision must be made between April 1, 2027, and the expiration date of that 3-year visa. If you miss that deadline, the calculation method changes.

The application method remains unchanged. Permanent residence applications must be submitted to the immigration office with jurisdiction over your place of residence; online applications are not accepted. The fee is only required after approval. For permanent residence applications accepted on or after October 1, 2026, the processing fee increases from 10,000 yen to 200,000 yen. For cases accepted on or before September 30, 2026, even if the approval date falls after October, the fee remains 10,000 yen. The fee is paid using revenue stamps at the counter after receiving the approval notice, at which point you will also receive your new residence card. If denied, you do not need to pay. The reduced fee of 20,000 yen is limited to specific statuses and those who meet criteria for financial hardship or humanitarian reasons. General applicants should budget for 200,000 yen to avoid being caught off guard when the notice arrives.

Your pension is also laid out for inspection. The authorities will estimate your future pension benefits and compare them against a benchmark—the amount you would receive assuming you worked for 30 years at a salary level matching your income and participated in the Employees' Pension. If the estimate is insufficient, you can supplement it with financial assets. The younger you are when applying, the lower the threshold for supplemental assets. For those with a spouse, it's judged based on your combined income. You might usually think of pension as just a deduction from your paycheck, but for a permanent residency application, it becomes a report card laid out on the table.

B1-level Japanese is now in the mix. For applications submitted on or after April 1, 2027, B1-level Japanese proficiency will be a factor for consideration. B1 refers to the proficiency level in the Japanese Language Education Framework. This does not apply to Highly Skilled Professionals and their families, those who have completed 6 years of education in Japan (elementary through high school), or children of permanent residents who meet certain criteria. The guidelines do not yet specify how to prove this. If you have Japanese language test scores, keep them safe, but do not assume they will be accepted or which specific tests will count until further announcements are made.

The remaining items are easily overlooked. Your understanding of Japan's systems and rules is also evaluated, based on the 'Guidebook for Living and Working,' and verified using methods designated by the Commissioner of the Immigration Services Agency. If you have school-age children, whether they are enrolled in school is also a factor. If you have been out of the country for more than six months at a time, or for a total of 2 years and 6 months or more within the past 10 years without a reasonable explanation, it will be viewed negatively. If you travel for business often, pull out your entry/exit records and calculate them to be sure.

Quick summary

  • Income is calculated based on the entire household. It must consistently exceed the average income of a Japanese household of the same size. Income from 'Designated Activities' (shikakugai katsudo) held by family members on a Dependent visa is not counted. Overseas dependents you support are included in the household count. Note that this is a factor for consideration, not a guaranteed threshold for approval.
  • The new guidelines generally apply to applications submitted on or after April 1, 2027. The income and 'no public burden' requirements will retroactively apply to applications submitted on or after April 1, 2026, that were still under review as of October 1, 2026. For the 3-year residency period transition arrangement, applications submitted before March 31, 2027, will still be treated as the maximum period; applications after that date must meet three specific conditions to qualify.
  • Permanent residence applications must be submitted in person at the immigration office. For applications accepted on or after October 1, 2026, the processing fee is 200,000 yen. Once you receive the approval notice, take it to the counter and pay using revenue stamps; you do not need to pay if the application is denied. Applications accepted on or before September 30 remain at 10,000 yen.
  • Pension is evaluated based on the gap between the projected future benefit and the benchmark. Shortfalls can be covered by financial assets; younger applicants have a lower threshold, and it's judged based on combined spousal income.
  • For applications after April 1, 2027, B1-level Japanese proficiency will be a consideration, excluding Highly Skilled Professionals and their families. The specific proof method has not yet been clarified, so keep your test results handy and wait for further announcements on the verification process.

From the editor-in-chief

Regarding the number 200,000, I thought there was an extra zero at first. I had 10,000 yen in my head, but when the notification arrived and I had to go to the counter to buy revenue stamps, I realized I was wrong. I was the one standing at the counter completely dazed. Just to be clear, you only pay once the permit is approved; if it's not approved, you don't pay. Don't rush to withdraw this money before you even apply.
