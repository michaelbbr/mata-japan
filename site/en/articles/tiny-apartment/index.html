A 9-square-meter room in Tokyo: you only know what "just right" means once you live in it

The floor area in advertisements is calculated using the wall-center method; the actual usable space is smaller. It is more useful to bring a tape measure when viewing a property than just looking at photos.

If you are in a hurry to head out, scroll straight down; the quick summary at the end collects everything you can follow step by step.

Apartments around 9 square meters are always on Tokyo rental sites. The photos look interesting, but before moving in, you have to think about whether your things will actually fit. What is the concept of this size? Rental ads calculate 1 jo (tatami mat) as 1.62 square meters or more, but properties just over 9 square meters usually include a bathroom, toilet, and kitchenette. The space you can actually use for a bed, desk, and luggage is smaller than the numbers suggest. Once you put in a single bed and a small desk, there is no room left to stand if you open your suitcase.

The gap in rent is so wide it feels like two different cities. According to At Home's rental statistics, the average advertised rent for a single-occupancy apartment under 30 square meters in Tokyo's 23 Wards in September 2026 was 114,460 yen, hitting a new high for consecutive months. At the same time, on Able's property page, a 9.91-square-meter 1R apartment in Honkomagome, Bunkyo Ward, was listed for 35,000 yen per month plus a 5,000 yen management fee—an old wooden apartment registered on November 11, 2026. However, these two figures cannot be directly compared. 114,460 yen is the overall average for properties under 30 square meters, while 35,000 yen is the asking price for a single, small, old unit. The age of the building, facilities, and actual floor space differ greatly, so you can't judge which is a better deal just by looking at the rent. The price of 'cheap' is written in the conditions: old age, small size, and basic facilities. The rent you save is essentially traded for living space.

The floor area figures themselves have their own mysteries. Rental advertisements use 'wall-center' (hekishin) measurements for exclusive area, starting from the center line of the wall thickness. The actual usable space for a person is the 'inner-face' (uchinori) inside the walls. Both registration and actual feel are based on the inner-face. Industry consensus is that the inner-face is about 5% to 8% smaller than the wall-center area. This is just a rough estimate; the actual difference depends on wall thickness and structure. For a normal house, this difference isn't noticeable, but for a 9-square-meter unit, a difference of about 0.7 square meters—roughly half a tatami mat—is the difference between whether a storage box will fit or not. When viewing a property, measure the inner width of the wall where the bed will go and compare it to the advertised numbers to have a better idea.

🏠 In this type of layout, you have to rotate the use of space to survive. Use the loft for sleeping, move the mattress up there, and leave the floor clear for a desk and chair. The trade-off is climbing a ladder every day, and in summer, heat rises, making the loft stuffier than the floor. Some small units use a three-piece unit bath where the bathroom, sink, and toilet share one space. There is limited room to move while showering, so check the shower position and drainage when viewing the property. If there is no storage furniture, stack upwards; use walls, behind doors, and under the bed. If you have too much stuff, it will overflow, so people living in these rooms usually pause for three seconds before buying anything to think about where to put it.

Who this suits is very clear. People with little luggage, who come home only to sleep and bathe, whose company or school is within walking distance or one train ride, and who spend the money saved on rent outside, can live this way very smoothly. People who work from home all day should think twice; the bed is right next to the desk, you finish a meeting and fall straight asleep, work and rest stick together, and after a few months you go dull. People who invite friends over, like to cook, and have a lot of clothes and books should not be lured away by low rent; after living there a while you may want to move, and once moving costs and initial fees are counted back, you have not saved anything at all.

Do one extra thing on viewing day: stand in the center of the room and turn in a circle. The walls your hands can reach are your whole world. If it still feels okay after that, then sign.

Quick summary

  • The advertised area is based on the wall-center measurement; the actual usable area is based on the inner-wall measurement, which is about 5% to 8% smaller (a rough estimate, depending on the structure). A 9-square-meter difference is about half a tatami mat.
  • The average advertised rent for a single-occupancy apartment under 30 square meters in Tokyo's 23 wards is 114,460 yen (as of September 2026). Older properties around 9 square meters can be as low as 40,000 yen, depending on the age and facilities.
  • Loft bed above, floor space for desk and chairs below; for a three-piece unit bath, check the shower position and drainage first.
  • It's not suitable for people who work from home, often invite friends over, or have a lot of belongings; the rent you save will be eaten up by moving costs
  • Bring a tape measure when viewing a property, measure the inner width of the wall where the bed will go, that's the most reliable check against the advertised figures

From the editor-in-chief

When I was viewing properties I brought a tape measure, and the agent watched me measure the walls with a slightly awkward expression. Only after measuring did I realize the numbers in the listing were not quite the same as the space I could actually stand in. Since then, when I look at property photos, I first look at where a suitcase would go; if it does not fit, it does not count.
