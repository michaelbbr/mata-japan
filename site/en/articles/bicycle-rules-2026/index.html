Bicycle blue tickets are in effect: that moment of using your phone while riding costs 12,000 yen

From April 1, 2026, applies to ages 16 and older, fines from 3000 to 12000 yen, amounts verified against the National Police Agency's bicycle rules handbook

This article has a lot of numbers; if you are short on time, scroll straight to the quick summary at the end, where the amounts and age thresholds are, and you can come back and read slowly later.

You're rushing out in the morning, riding your bike to the station, and your phone vibrates in your pocket—many people reach for it immediately. Starting April 1, 2026, if you take your phone out to talk or stare at the screen while riding, the price could be 12,000 yen. Bicycles are now included in the traffic violation notification system, commonly known as the 'blue ticket' system. If a rider aged 16 or older commits a listed violation and is reported, they must pay a fine ranging from 3,000 to 12,000 yen, covering a total of 113 types of behavior (in practice, the focus is initially on guidance and warnings, with blue tickets targeting malicious or dangerous riding). It doesn't matter if you have a driver's license or not; whether you're riding a 'mama-chari' to buy groceries, a road bike to commute, or taking your child to daycare, the same system applies.

🚲 The old system for handling bicycle violations was awkward. Previously, bicycles were not part of the fine system, so if a violation was reported it went straight to the criminal procedure of a red ticket; the procedure was heavy, and once sent to prosecutors it often ended in non-prosecution, with the result that people could neither be fined nor deterred. The blue ticket fills in this middle part: pay the fine within the deadline and the case ends, without a criminal trial and without leaving a criminal record. That deadline is within 7 days starting the day after you receive the blue ticket; take the payment slip to a bank or post office to pay. People under 16 are not subject to blue tickets and in principle are still handled with guidance and warnings; high school students aged 16 and over are held to the same standard as adults, which families with high school students should explain especially clearly.

The amounts are based on the official table, focusing on the items residents are most likely to encounter. Using a mobile phone to talk or staring at the screen while cycling is 12,000 yen, the most expensive category on the entire table. Forcing your way through a railroad crossing after the gate has lowered is 7,000 yen. Running a red light is 6,000 yen; traffic violations such as riding on the right side of the lane or riding on sidewalks without bicycle access signs are also 6,000 yen. Failing to stop at an intersection where you should, not turning on lights at night, riding while holding an umbrella, or wearing headphones and being unable to hear surroundings—these violations of Public Safety Commission rules are all 5,000 yen. Poor maintenance, such as broken brakes, is also 5,000 yen. Riding with two people or riding side-by-side is 3,000 yen. There is an exception for carrying children: if the rider is 16 or older and uses a compliant child seat, they can carry children under 6—one in the front seat and one in the back, for a maximum of two (subject to weight and other conditions). This legal method of carrying children is not considered 'riding with two people' as mentioned here; check the official rulebook for details.

The everyday picture of enforcement is stated clearly by officials. General violations are mainly handled with guidance and warnings on the spot. The blue ticket is for malicious and dangerous riding, such as forcing pedestrians to stop suddenly, forcing other vehicles to brake suddenly, or continuing to ride after being warned. Riding after drinking and riding while drunk are more serious; these two are not in blue ticket scope and go directly to the red ticket criminal process, and using a phone while riding in a way that endangers traffic is the same. So the introduction of blue tickets does not mean police will now stand at every intersection issuing tickets; it means those truly dangerous ways of riding can now actually be fined.

There is also a very practical warning. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's official page specifically warns that there have been scams where fake police officers collect money on the spot in the name of blue tickets (traffic violation notices). Real police officers will not collect traffic fines at the scene of a violation. Fines are paid by taking a payment slip to a bank or post office. If someone on the side of the road asks you to take out cash on the spot, call 110 first.

Life goes on, you just need to change a few riding habits. Keep your phone in your bag, and if you need to check the map, pull over to the side. Turn on your bike light before heading out at night, and at the 'Stop' sign at intersections, put your feet down and come to a complete stop. These are things you should be doing for safe cycling anyway. Now that the blue ticket system is in effect, some traffic violations have clear fines. If you do the math, the cost of one ticket for using your phone is enough to buy a very good bike light plus a phone mount.

Quick summary

  • From April 1, 2026, the blue ticket system applies to bicycle riders aged 16 and older, including high school students who have turned 16
  • Using a phone while cycling 12000 yen, forcing a level crossing 7000 yen, running a red light and riding against traffic 6000 yen
  • Failing to stop momentarily, not turning on lights, riding with an umbrella, and faulty brakes: 5000 yen; riding with a passenger and riding side by side: 3000 yen
  • If you pay the fine within the deadline, the case is closed and no criminal record is left. Riding a bicycle under the influence is not covered by the blue ticket system and goes through the red ticket criminal procedure.
  • Police will not collect money on the spot; someone at the roadside asking you to pay cash on the spot is a scam, call 110
