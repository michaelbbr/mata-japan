How to read supermarket half-price stickers; the 500-yen-off sticker is the easiest to misread.

Evening discount patrol: read the stickers before you buy

People rushing to buy groceries in the evening do not need to read the whole article; there is a quick summary at the end, read that before entering the supermarket.

Walking into a Japanese supermarket in the evening, the prepared foods section smells of freshly fried food, and in front there is always a small group of people moving slowly, their eyes scanning like mine detectors. They are waiting for the moment a staff member appears pushing the sticker cart.

After living in Japan for a long time, you will find that discount stickers are a language you need to learn. 'Hangaku' means the price is cut exactly in half, 'san-wari-biki' is a 30% discount, and 'go-hyaku-en-biki' is 500 yen off the original price. People often mistake these for 50% off, only to find out at checkout that it wasn't that cheap. I have misread them before and only realized it when I checked the receipt at home. Some stickers have the new price printed directly on them; the price written is already the discounted price. If you subtract it again yourself, you will end up having a little internal drama at the checkout. Prepared food bought at the supermarket to take home is usually subject to the 8% reduced tax rate. Some price tags have the tax-excluded price printed in large font and the tax-included price in small print next to it. Make sure to identify the tax-included price before checking out so you don't calculate it wrong.

🏷️ Next to the sticker, you will often see 'honjitsu-chu' (within today) or a consumption deadline for today. For prepared foods and bento boxes, buy only what you can eat that night. Cheap as they are, they are not meant for stocking up for a week. The Consumer Affairs Agency's explanation on food date labeling reminds us that the 'consumption deadline' (use-by date) and 'best-before date' are two different things; do not take risks with items past their consumption deadline. The money you save on half-price items is not worth the cost of a doctor's visit.

I decide what to eat tonight before I leave home, then go wait for the stickers, rather than letting the stickers decide dinner. For raw foods like sushi and sashimi, I only buy as much as can be finished tonight. For fried foods and simmered dishes, only if the use-by date has not passed and they can be refrigerated properly do I consider taking an extra portion for tomorrow's bento. Anything marked with a same-day use-by date gets finished that day. After shopping at the same store for a long time, I have gradually figured out roughly when it puts out the first round of stickers and when it goes to half price. That timetable will not appear in any tourist guide; it is something people who live here learn by pushing a shopping basket around, lap after lap.

Now my evening loop feels like a stroll, and I bring dinner home along the way.

Quick summary

  • Han-gaku means half price, san-wari-biki means 30 percent off, and go-hyaku-en-biki means 500 yen off directly.
  • Items with a new price printed on the sticker are already at the discounted price; do not deduct it again
  • For items marked 'today' as the expiration date, eat them tonight; do not stockpile.
  • Only buy enough fresh food to finish tonight; for fried or stewed dishes, only buy extra for tomorrow's bento if the consumption deadline hasn't passed and it can be properly refrigerated.

Source materials

  • Consumer Affairs Agency food date labeling guide: 'Consumption deadline' and 'Best-before date' have different definitions. Judgment of the deadline should be based on the label type and storage conditions; this article's deadline reminders are written accordingly.
  • Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries and Consumer Affairs Agency food loss estimation data: 'mikiri' (discounted) sales are one of the ways retailers reduce food waste. This article does not cite specific discount times for individual stores; store times are subject to on-site conditions.

From the editor-in-chief
