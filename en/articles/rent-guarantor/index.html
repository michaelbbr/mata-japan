Foreigners keep getting rejected when renting? First understand what the 'guarantor company' screening is blocking

Not having a Japanese guarantor doesn't mean you can't find a place to live

"Sorry, this property does not accept foreigners." Foreigners who have looked for housing in Tokyo may be familiar with that sentence. The reasons for rejection are not always the same. Some landlords worry about a tenant's ability to pay or about communication issues, and some properties simply restrict foreign tenants. When this happens, in addition to checking the property's conditions, you should also understand what the guarantor company is actually screening at its stage of the process.

The core of the trust mechanism for renting in Japan is the 'guarantor.' Previously, this relied on a joint guarantor (usually a Japanese relative), but now the mainstream has shifted to rent debt guarantee companies: you pay a guarantee commission fee, and the company covers your rent if you fall behind, handling the subsequent process. However, the guarantee company paying your rent does not mean the tenant is off the hook; you may still be required to repay the debt. For foreigners without Japanese relatives, the guarantee company essentially converts the risk of 'the landlord doesn't know you' into a clear, upfront fee.

Take GTN (Global Trust Networks), which specializes in foreigners, as an example. Since its founding in 2006 it has only done foreigner support. In addition to rent guarantees, it also offers rental brokerage, telecommunications, and employment support, with multilingual service. For people who have just arrived and whose Japanese is not yet fluent, being able to communicate in Chinese during the screening stage makes a very big difference. As for fees, the initial guarantee fee varies by property and guarantor company. Using Shinshu University's published GTN plan materials as an example, the initial guarantee fee is 30%–100% of total rent, with a minimum fee threshold and an annual guarantee fee as well. These are the conditions of a specific plan; the actual amount is still subject to the estimate provided by the agency. Before signing, be sure to have the agency write out both the initial guarantee fee and the annual guarantee fee clearly; do not look only at the first one.

🏠 When looking for a place, filter on SUUMO/HOME'S first, or look for agencies that explicitly state they work with foreigners to save time being rejected repeatedly. If your application is rejected, ask the agent if they know why—it could be your period of stay, proof of income, or the property's specific requirements. However, landlords don't always disclose the reason, so ask the agent to help you find a property with more suitable conditions. Prepare for initial costs of about 4–6 months' rent (deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantor fee, fire insurance, plus the first month's rent). Having the money ready gives you more confidence during the screening process.

The real reason for being rejected is rarely explicitly stated by the screening party. It could be due to income or contact verification issues, but you can't rule out the possibility of being blocked simply for being a foreigner—there's no point in lying to yourself about that. What you can control is having proof of stable income and a reliable contact/guarantor system ready before you go house hunting. At the very least, you won't lose out because of your paperwork.

I didn't sleep well that night, over a home I hadn't even rented yet.
