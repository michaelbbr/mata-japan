Foreigners keep getting rejected when renting? First understand what the 'guarantor company' screening is blocking

Not having a Japanese guarantor doesn't mean you can't find a place to live

"Sorry, this property does not accept foreigners." Foreigners who have looked for housing in Tokyo may be familiar with that sentence. The reasons for rejection are not always the same. Some landlords worry about a tenant's ability to pay or about communication issues, and some properties simply restrict foreign tenants. When this happens, in addition to checking the property's conditions, you should also understand what the guarantor company is actually screening at its stage of the process.

The trust mechanism of renting in Japan is, at its core, “guarantee”. In the past it relied on a joint guarantor (usually a Japanese relative), but now the mainstream has changed to 家賃債務保證 companies: you pay a guarantee commission fee, and the company advances the rent when you are in arrears and handles the follow-up. But the guarantee company advancing rent does not mean the tenant does not have to repay the money; afterwards you may still be required to repay the arrears. For foreigners without Japanese relatives, a guarantee company actually converts the risk of “the landlord does not know you” directly into a clear fee.

Take GTN (Global Trust Networks), which specializes in foreigners, as an example. Since its founding in 2006 it has only done foreigner support. In addition to rent guarantees, it also offers rental brokerage, telecommunications, and employment support, with multilingual service. For people who have just arrived and whose Japanese is not yet fluent, being able to communicate in Chinese during the screening stage makes a very big difference. As for fees, the initial guarantee fee varies by property and guarantor company. Using Shinshu University's published GTN plan materials as an example, the initial guarantee fee is 30%–100% of total rent, with a minimum fee threshold and an annual guarantee fee as well. These are the conditions of a specific plan; the actual amount is still subject to the estimate provided by the agency. Before signing, be sure to have the agency write out both the initial guarantee fee and the annual guarantee fee clearly; do not look only at the first one.

🏠 When looking for a property, filter first on SUUMO / HOME'S, or go directly to an agent that advertises support for foreigners, to save the time of being turned down one by one. If your application is rejected, you can first ask the agent whether they know the reason, for example your period of stay, proof of income, or the property's own occupancy conditions. However, the screening side may not explain the reason, in which case you can ask the agent to help find a property with conditions that suit you better. For initial costs, prepare yourself mentally for 4 to 6 months' rent (deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantee fee, fire insurance); with the money ready, you will feel more confident during screening.

When looking for a home in Japan, whether your Japanese is fluent or not is actually secondary. What landlords and screening companies care about most is stable proof of income and a guarantor system with someone who can be contacted. Prepare these documents first before viewing properties, and your success rate will be much higher.

From the editor-in-chief

During the time I was looking for a home, I got nervous whenever my phone rang, afraid it was a confirmation call from the guarantor company. It happened to call while I was in the shower; I called back with my hair still wet, and the other party said they had already finished work for the day and would call again tomorrow. I didn't sleep well that night, over a home I hadn't even rented yet.
