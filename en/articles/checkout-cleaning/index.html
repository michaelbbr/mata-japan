Charged 150,000 yen when moving out, and the most baffling part was the 20,000 yen for changing the lock

After living there for a full 6 years, the residual value of wallpaper is only 1 yen, and in principle the cost of changing the lock shouldn't fall on you either

This article is a bit long. If you are short on time, scroll straight to the bottom for the quick summary.

Recently a move-out settlement statement has been circulating in the Chinese-speaking community, a case posted by a netizen. Cleaning fee 50,000, wallpaper 80,000, lock change 20,000, total 150,000 yen; the original post on X was viewed more than nine million times, and the comment section was full of people who had been charged similar amounts. Of the three charges, the most puzzling is that 20,000 for the lock change. If every key is returned without a single one missing, why should the money for changing the lock still be counted on the tenant?

For this lock change cost, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism's 原狀回復ガイドライン places it on the landlord's side. Changing the lock is for the safety and management of the next resident, and is originally a cost the landlord should bear, on the condition that the key has not been lost or damaged. If you return all the keys and a lock change fee still appears on the statement, you can ask the other party on the spot what the reason is.

The 80,000 yen for wallpaper is calculated differently. The ガイドライン set the useful life of wallpaper at 6 years; after living there for a full 6 years, the residual value drops to 1 yen, and after 4 years, only about one-third remains. But the residual value of the wallpaper is not equal to the entire construction cost; it still depends on how the breakdown is calculated. Even if a stain on the wall really was caused by you, in principle what can be charged to you is the one wall where the damaged part is located. Charging you for replacing everything in the whole room and sending you the bill doesn't hold up. Sun fading, furniture indentations, and small nail holes left by hanging pictures are all marks left by normal use. Those are the landlord's responsibility.

Article 621 of the Civil Code was written into the law after the 2020 revision: wear and tear from normal use and 経年変化 from the passage of time are not part of the tenant's obligation to restore the property to its original condition. The ガイドライン mentioned above has no legal binding force, but courts and consumer affairs centers both use it as a yardstick when handling disputes.

Whether you have to pay the 50,000 yen cleaning fee depends on the contract. Full-property cleaning done for the next tenant is, in principle, the landlord's cost. If the lease's special terms clearly state a fixed cleaning fee and it was explained when you signed, that special term may be valid, but it still depends on whether the agreement is written clearly and whether the fee is reasonable. You can't say there's nothing to negotiate just because it's written in the contract. Conversely, if the special terms field is blank and the amount was quoted on the spot by the other party, then there is room to negotiate. Oil stains you left yourself and mold that grew because you left it unattended are a different matter. That's a problem with how you used the property, and it's quicker to accept it.

🧾 With a statement like this, staring at the total is useless. Lay out the details and check them item by item: unit price, number of square meters or tatami mats, and scope of work. Match the years you lived there against the useful life and calculate the residual value yourself once. Take out the special clauses page of the contract and photograph it, reading line by line what you actually signed at the time. If you cannot reach agreement, call the consumer hotline 188, which connects you to your local consumer affairs center, and take the settlement statement and contract with you to talk; the staff there are very familiar with cases like this.

People who have not moved yet will run into this on the day they sign the contract. Turn to the cleaning fee clause in the special terms and make clear on the spot whether the amount is fixed in writing and whether anyone explained it when signing; this clause will come back for you on move-out day. On the day you move in, photograph the whole room, do not miss a single shot of the walls, floor or kitchen counter, and keep the dates on the photos. On move-out inspection day, the management company's person photographs each wall with a tablet while you stand beside them watching; if you know where you stand, your expression will not change. If there are items on the settlement statement you do not understand or think are wrong, do not sign your agreement on site; take the statement home first, check it, and then reply.

Quick summary

  • Lock change fee: if the key is not lost or damaged, in principle the landlord pays
  • Wallpaper: useful life of 6 years; after living there for a full 6 years, the residual value is 1 yen; in principle, calculated based on the one wall where the damaged area is located
  • Sun fading, furniture indentations, and nail holes are normal use, and under Civil Code Article 621 they are not part of the tenant's restoration obligation
  • Cleaning fees depend on the special terms: if the amount is fixed in writing and was explained, it's mostly valid; if the special terms are blank and the price was quoted on the spot, you can negotiate
  • If you can't reach an agreement, call the consumer hotline 188, and bring the settlement statement and contract to a consumer affairs center
