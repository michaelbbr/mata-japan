A day working at a game company in Japan: arrived at the office at 9:50, left at 7:07

A programmer's day is mostly spent writing code and attending meetings; the job title of 'planner' has its own specific meaning in the Japanese gaming industry.

People who want to work in the Japanese gaming industry usually have an idealized image in their heads. Paolo fromTOKYO filmed a programmer working at Bandai Namco Studios in 2019, and the video has accumulated about 17.54 million views. The day shown in the video is quite different from that idealized image. The interviewee was 23, woke up at 8:15 AM, lived alone in Tokyo, washed his face, got dressed, left at 9:10 AM to take the train, and arrived at the office around 9:50 AM (working hours vary by company; this was just that engineer's routine). The company building has several floors, with about a thousand people working inside.

What happens after entering the office is very ordinary. Clock in, sit down, turn on the computer, change into slippers, and look at emails and internal company messages while eating breakfast. The morning is almost all programming. At 12, go out for lunch with colleagues who joined the company at the same time; besides restaurants, food trucks also rotate in downstairs. Return to your seat at 1 in the afternoon and keep programming; at 2 there is a stand-up meeting of about 30 minutes, where the team goes through what they did yesterday, what they will do today, and where they are stuck, looking at work items projected on the screen one by one. Once every two weeks there is also a sprint retrospective meeting.

That day he left the company at 19:07. Counting from when he entered at 9:50 in the morning, including lunch and meetings, he spent more than nine hours in the office. He said himself that when a big project needs rushing, overtime still happens. In the video, other teams in the same building were also busy. One action RPG took about five years from planning, with about 200 people working on it at peak, and after launch it still needs updates and additional content based on player feedback. That is how the time from a game's start to reaching players adds up. 🎮

Job titles can also be misleading. In the Japanese gaming industry, the term game designer is rarely used directly. Those in design roles are usually called planners, or game planners. In another behind-the-scenes video, the lead planner of a mobile game company stated clearly that a planner's job is not just about coming up with ideas, but also writing proposals and specification documents so the whole team understands what to build. Communication and organization take up a huge part of the work. When job seekers from Chinese-speaking regions browse listings, they often see the word planner and mistake it for a marketing planner, which can easily lead to applying for the wrong department.

For a sense of the industry's scale, compare CESA's figures. The CESA Game Industry Report 2025 estimates the global game content market in 2024 at about 31 trillion yen, with overall employment in Japan's domestic game industry at about 200,000 people, of which core companies directly doing development and publishing employ about 58,000 to 83,000 people. The market is not small, but people who want to enter this industry, besides looking at works and company reputation, also need to look at actual job content and working hours.

Another developer survey by CESA provides a snapshot of working hours and income. The 2025 survey received 339 valid responses, with 87.0% being regular employees. The average annual personal income of respondents was approximately 6.74 million yen, with the largest group falling in the 5 million yen range. A quick reminder: the sample size is only 339 people, so these are figures from respondents, not the industry average. Average weekly working hours are 44.40 hours during normal periods and 52.53 hours during busy periods. That 8-hour difference between 44 and 52 hours is what accumulates during the crunch time before a product launch. While the average annual income looks decent, once you look at the distribution, nearly half of the people earn 6 million yen or less. If you use the average as your own expectation, you might be in for a surprise.

Looking at the day as a whole—commuting, stand-up meetings, coding, and rushing to meet deadlines—the rhythm isn't much different from any other IT company. The difference lies in the product itself. A single line of code you change might end up as content that players actually interact with, and what you discuss in the meeting room eventually becomes a game in someone else's hands. For those looking to enter this industry, the only question left is whether you enjoy this kind of time-intensive work. Of course, this is just one engineer's day; it doesn't mean every company is like this.
