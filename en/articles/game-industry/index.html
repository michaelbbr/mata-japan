A day working at a game company in Japan: arrived at the office at 9:50, left at 7:07

A programmer's day is mostly writing code and attending meetings, and the job title 企劃 has its own meaning in Japan's game industry

People who want to go to Japan to work in games mostly have a picture in their mind. Paolo fromTOKYO followed a programmer working at Bandai Namco Studios; this video has accumulated about 17.54 million views, and the day in it is some distance from that imagined picture. The interviewee is 23 years old, gets up at 8:15 in the morning, lives alone in Tokyo, washes his face, changes his clothes, leaves at 9:10 to take the train, and enters the office at around 9:50 (work hour systems vary by company; this is just that engineer's own routine). The company occupies several floors of a whole building, with about a thousand people working inside.

What happens after entering the office is very ordinary. Clock in, sit down, turn on the computer, change into slippers, and look at emails and internal company messages while eating breakfast. The morning is almost all programming. At 12, go out for lunch with colleagues who joined the company at the same time; besides restaurants, food trucks also rotate in downstairs. Return to your seat at 1 in the afternoon and keep programming; at 2 there is a stand-up meeting of about 30 minutes, where the team goes through what they did yesterday, what they will do today, and where they are stuck, looking at work items projected on the screen one by one. Once every two weeks there is also a sprint retrospective meeting.

That day he left the company at 19:07. Counting from when he entered at 9:50 in the morning, including lunch and meetings, he spent more than nine hours in the office. He said himself that when a big project needs rushing, overtime still happens. In the video, other teams in the same building were also busy. One action RPG took about five years from planning, with about 200 people working on it at peak, and after launch it still needs updates and additional content based on player feedback. That is how the time from a game's start to reaching players adds up. 🎮

Job titles are also easy to misunderstand. Japan's game industry rarely uses the term game designer directly; people in design positions are mostly called 企劃, that is, game planners. In another follow-up video, the chief planner at a mobile game company put it plainly: a planner needs not only to come up with ideas, but also to write ideas into proposals and specification documents so the whole team understands what to make; communication and organizing make up a large part of the work. When Chinese-speaking job seekers browse vacancies and see the word 企劃, they think it's marketing planning, and applying to the wrong department is entirely possible.

For a sense of the industry's scale, compare CESA's figures. The CESA Game Industry Report 2025 estimates the global game content market in 2024 at about 31 trillion yen, with overall employment in Japan's domestic game industry at about 200,000 people, of which core companies directly doing development and publishing employ about 58,000 to 83,000 people. The market is not small, but people who want to enter this industry, besides looking at works and company reputation, also need to look at actual job content and working hours.

Another CESA developer survey outlines working hours and income. The 2025 survey collected 339 valid responses, 87.0% from full-time employees, with average personal annual income of about 6.74 million yen and the largest group clustered around 5 million yen. Average weekly working hours were 44.40 hours in normal periods and 52.53 hours in busy periods. The 8-hour gap between 44 hours and 52 hours comes from the days leading up to a release. Average annual income looks decent, but when you spread out the distribution, nearly half earn under 6 million yen, so if you take the average as your own expectation, the gap can be large.

Breaking this day down, commuting, stand-up meetings, writing code, rushing deadlines, the rhythm is not that different from a general IT company. The difference is the product itself. Change one line of code, and in the end it may become content that players actually come into contact with, and what is discussed in the meeting room will also become a game in someone else's hands. For people who want to enter this industry, what remains is whether they themselves like this kind of work that piles up time.
