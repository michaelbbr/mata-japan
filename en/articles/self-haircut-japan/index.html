People who cut their own hair in Japan because they are afraid of miscommunication

Afraid of miscommunicating with the stylist, so I bought electric clippers. It saves not just money, but also those ten-plus minutes of sitting in a chair trying to explain what I want with hand gestures.

For those who want to be quick, there are three Japanese phrases at the end of this article you can use directly at the salon. The main text explains why some people prefer to do it themselves in the bathroom. Living in Japan, the simple act of getting a haircut is surprisingly a test of your Japanese. Once you're in the chair, the stylist asks about length, bangs, and whether you want the sides buzzed. You understand the gist of every sentence, but when you put it all together, you're terrified of nodding at the wrong thing. I asked around about people's experiences with communication mishaps at Japanese salons, and the answer was blunt: I'm scared, so I just cut it myself. 😅

The gear for cutting your own hair is actually quite simple. An adjustable electric clipper, a few guide combs of different lengths, and a hand mirror to see the back of your head are enough to get started. The back of the head is the hardest part of the whole head; you can't see it and have to rely on your sense of touch and the hand mirror. Most first-timers spend the longest time there. Spread some newspaper on the bathroom floor, wrap it up and throw it away when you're done; cleaning up is easier than you'd think. If you mess up, no one will see it—just wear a hat for a few days. There's a simple trick for length: use a shorter guide comb for the sides, one size longer for the top, and slightly lift the clipper as you move across the transition area so the marks aren't as obvious. If you're just starting out, it's better to trim it in two sessions than to shave it all off in one go; you can't glue hair back on once it's cut.

The math actually makes sense. Chain quick-cut salons are a good benchmark. The current official price at QB HOUSE is 1,400 yen (tax included), raised from 1,350 yen on February 1, 2025. This covers only a haircut—no shampoo, no blow-dry, no shaving—and they use a vacuum to suck up the loose hair afterward. You buy a ticket and wait in line; the haircut itself takes about 10 minutes, and the wait time depends on how busy they are. Cutting it yourself means saving that 1,400 yen plus the time spent traveling back and forth. If you cut your hair ten times a year, the cost of the electric clippers pays for itself in no time.

Before entering a shop, there's one more thing that often leaves people stumped: what's the difference between a barbershop and a hair salon? According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's regulations, barbers and beauticians are governed by different laws. Both can cut hair, but one of the main differences lies in facial shaving. Using a razor for a full face shave is, in principle, the work of a barber, while beauticians can only lightly trim fine hairs on the face as part of makeup application. If you want a haircut and a face shave at the same time, look for a shop with a barber sign and a rotating pole.

Once you decide to actually walk into a shop, you don't need much Japanese; knowing how to talk about length is enough for most of it. "Zentai-teki ni mijikame ni shite kudasai" means to make it a bit shorter overall, and "Yoko wa kariage de, ue wa sukoshi nokoshite kudasai" means to trim the sides short and leave a bit on top. When you're unsure, just show them a photo on your phone and add, "Shashin mitai na kanji de onegaishimasu." Once you've said that, sit back and leave the rest to the scissors. The communication barrier is actually much lower than you'd expect, but like my friend who cuts his own hair, many people spend years before realizing that.
