For your first run at the Imperial Palace, remember first: this 5 km route goes counterclockwise

Pedestrians have priority around the Imperial Palace. Even if you run fast, don't take the path from them

Foreigners running around the Imperial Palace outer garden for the first time are usually startled near Takebashi: the sidewalk is so wide, why is everyone as if pulled by the same line, all running in the same direction?

I have run this route for many years. The answer is simple: counterclockwise!

The outer loop running route around the Imperial Palace is about 5 kilometers per lap, with no traffic lights to wait for along the way. For people living in the capital region who can only squeeze onto trains to commute on weekdays, it's the most luxurious stretch of road on a weekend morning. But this is also a public space shared by pedestrians, tourists, and bicycles, so Chiyoda Ward and related organizations established the "Etiquette for Using Paths Around the Imperial Palace" long ago: pedestrians first, don't block the path by running side by side, form a single file in narrow places, don't use your phone while running, run the loop counterclockwise, and take your trash with you. The Ministry of the Environment's Imperial Palace Outer Garden Management Office has separate "regional rules" for group activities of 20 or more people, which likewise clearly state to run the loop counterclockwise and prohibit overtaking in narrow sections.

For people who live here, these rules have long become an unspoken understanding every morning. Runners all go counterclockwise, so they're less likely to run into each other head-on. But there are also walkers and tourists on the road, so you still have to slow down when you encounter pedestrians. You slow down in front of Sakurada Gate, and the people behind you won't honk at you. There are no horns here, only footsteps.

🏃 If it is your first time here, just remember a few things: you can start from near Takebashi or Sakuradamon; first check the direction runners are moving, then join from the side. This route has no fixed starting point, so there is no need to go out of your way against the flow to find a start line. Around Takebashi, Hibiya, and Hanzomon there are paid Running Stations where you can store your things and shower, which is convenient if you go straight by train after work to change. On weekday evenings from 18:00 to 21:00 there are also many people running after work; if you want a more relaxed run, try early on a weekend morning. After your run, do not collapse on the path to stretch; move to Gaien Plaza or a wider spot by the roadside, which is also part of the etiquette.

But you have to run counterclockwise in the correct direction first before you are allowed to come and report your time to me. After running for a while, you learn that running in Tokyo is also about learning to share the same road with others.

From the editor-in-chief

The first time I ran around the Imperial Palace, I saw everyone going in the same direction and thought the course had been booked for an event, and I almost ran clockwise, saved by a look from someone beside me. After one lap I understood: you do not need to memorize the rules of this route, just follow the flow of people, except I was following it while panting hard.
