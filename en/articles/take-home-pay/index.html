Monthly salary of 300,000 yen: where did the more than 60,000 yen deducted on the payslip go?

Estimate for Tokyo, under 40, single: health insurance plus pension already account for more than half, take-home pay is about 237,000 yen

A monthly salary of 300,000 yen sounds like a nice round number, but what actually hits your account will be less. Open your pay stub, and you'll see a column of deductions—each one small, but they add up to a significant amount. To calculate this with a clear premise: working in Tokyo, under 40, single, no dependents, 300,000 yen monthly salary (excluding bonuses and overtime for now), assuming the standard monthly remuneration falls into the 300,000 yen bracket (actual brackets are determined by remuneration from April to June, including commuting allowances), and estimating resident tax based on the previous year's income. If the conditions change, the numbers will change too; I'll talk about that at the end.

Social insurance is the big one; the three parts add up to 44,070 yen. Health insurance follows the rate of the Kyokai Kenpo Tokyo Branch, which is 9.85% for the 2026 fiscal year. Starting from April 2026 (payable in May), an additional 0.23% for the Children and Child-rearing Contribution is added, totaling 10.08%. The company and the employee each pay half, so the employee pays 15,120 yen. The Employees' Pension Insurance rate is a uniform 18.3% nationwide, also split in half, with the employee paying 27,450 yen—the pension alone is nearly 30,000 yen. The employee's share for employment insurance for general businesses in the 2026 fiscal year is 0.5% of the total salary, which comes to 1,500 yen. 💴

Income tax is withheld at the source, deducted from your salary every month. The deduction is based on the National Tax Agency's monthly table. After deducting social insurance, for an amount of 255,930 yen, under Column A with 0 dependents, the trial calculation is 6,320 yen. The monthly deduction is just an estimate; at the end of the year, the company performs the year-end tax adjustment to settle any overpayments or underpayments.

Juminzei (residence tax) is the most confusing, as it is a tax on the previous year's income. If your annual income was 3.6 million yen the previous year, your employment income would be 2.44 million yen. After deducting social insurance premiums and the 430,000 yen basic deduction for juminzei, your taxable income is about 1.48 million yen. With a 10% income levy minus adjustment deductions, plus the per capita levy and the forest environmental tax, it comes to about 150,600 yen per year, averaging about 12,550 yen per month. The payment slip for June will be slightly higher, as the remainder is squeezed into the first month. Those who have just arrived in Japan and did not have taxable income in Japan the previous year will usually not have this deduction in their first year; if you meet the tax requirements the following year, the deduction may start in June, which is when many people realize their paycheck has become thinner.

Adding it all up—44,070 + 6,320 + 12,550—means 62,940 yen is deducted, leaving you with a take-home pay of about 237,060 yen, which is roughly 79% of your gross monthly salary. When I was young and looked at my pay stub for the first time, I stared at the pension line for a long time: 27,450 yen, enough for several decent lunches. I later learned that the 27,450 yen deducted from my pay was only my share; the company pays an equal amount on top of that. Pay stubs usually only list the amount deducted from your own salary. Also, eligible non-Japanese nationals who leave Japan and lose their domestic address can apply for the 'Lump-sum Withdrawal Payment.' In principle, you must apply within 2 years of losing your domestic address. Eligibility and the amount depend on your pension contribution history, and it is not a full refund of all premiums paid.

Calculation errors hide in several places. Income from April to June affects the standard monthly remuneration bracket applied for the following year; higher overtime pay during this period can increase your social insurance premiums later. Overtime pay affects the calculation of insurance premiums and income tax. Commuting allowances generally affect social insurance and employment insurance calculations, but portions that meet tax-exemption criteria are usually not subject to income tax. Bonuses have their own separate deduction methods. After turning 40, health insurance includes an additional 1.62% for nursing care insurance, which, after being split in half, results in an extra deduction of about 2,430 yen per month. Once factors like the number of dependents and housing loan deductions are added, the tax amount changes again. When your payslip doesn't add up, check these items one by one in this order, and the problem will usually surface.

Quick summary

  • The calculation assumes a monthly salary of 300,000 yen, living in Tokyo, under 40 years old, single with no dependents. The take-home pay is approximately 237,060 yen, which is about 79% of the monthly salary.
  • The three social insurance payments total 44,070 yen, with the Employees' Pension Insurance being the largest at 27,450 yen. The company actually pays the other half.
  • For health insurance, the Kyokai Kenpo Tokyo rate is 9.85 percent plus a 0.23 percent support contribution; after splitting the cost, the employee pays 15,120 yen.
  • Residence tax is based on the previous year's income and is about 150,600 yen per year; those working in Japan for their first year are not charged this.
  • Once you turn 40, you add the nursing care insurance premium, which increases your monthly deduction by about 2,430 yen after splitting the cost.
