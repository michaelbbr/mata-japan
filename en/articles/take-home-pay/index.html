Monthly salary of 300,000 yen: where did the more than 60,000 yen deducted on the payslip go?

Estimate for Tokyo, under 40, single: health insurance plus pension already account for more than half, take-home pay is about 237,000 yen

A monthly salary of 300,000 yen sounds like a round number, but what actually lands in your bank account is noticeably less. Open up the payslip and the deductions column has several lines. Each one isn't large on its own, but together they add up to a substantial amount. Let's calculate using a clearly defined set of assumptions: working in Tokyo, under 40, single, no dependents, monthly salary of 300,000 yen, bonuses and overtime not included this time, 標準報酬月額 taken as 300,000 yen, and 住民稅 estimated assuming the previous year's income was the same. Change one condition and the numbers move with it, which we'll cover at the end.

Social insurance is the big part, the three items adding up to 44,070 yen. Health insurance uses the rate of 協会けんぽ Tokyo branch, 9.85% in Reiwa 8, plus from April 2026 the child and childcare support contribution of 0.23%, for a total of 10.08%, split half and half between company and employee, with the employee paying 15,120 yen. The 厚生年金 rate is uniform nationwide at 18.3%, also split in half, with the employee paying 27,450 yen, nearly 30,000 yen for pension alone. For 雇用保険 in Reiwa 8, the insured person's share for general businesses is 5 per thousand, calculated on total salary, with the employee paying 1,500 yen. 💴

Income tax is withheld at source, pre-deducted from salary every month. The method follows the National Tax Agency's monthly table: take the amount after deducting social insurance, 255,930 yen, and look up the 甲欄 column, the cell for 0 dependents, and in this trial calculation it is about 6,330 yen. What is deducted each month is only an estimate; at year-end the company does 年末調整, refunding excess and collecting shortfalls, and the year is settled there.

住民稅 is the most confusing, because it bills the previous year's account. If the previous year's annual income was also 3,600,000 yen, employment income is 2,440,000 yen. After deducting social insurance premiums and the 住民稅 basic deduction of 430,000 yen, taxable income is about 1,480,000 yen. The 所得割 at 10%, minus the adjustment deduction, plus the 均等割 and 森林環境稅, comes to about 150,600 yen a year, averaging about 12,550 yen per month. The June bill will be slightly higher, with the fractional amounts all crowded into the first month. People who have just come to Japan and had no income subject to 住民稅 in Japan in the previous year usually do not have this deduction in the first year; if they meet the taxation conditions the next year, it may start being deducted from June, and many people discover their pay has thinned in that month.

Adding it all up, 44,070 plus 6,330 plus 12,550 equals 62,950 yen deducted, leaving take-home pay of about 237,050 yen, roughly 79% of monthly salary. When I was young and looked at a payslip seriously for the first time, I also stared at the pension line for a long time: 27,450 yen, enough for several decent lunches. Only later did I understand that the 27,450 yen deducted on the payslip is the portion I bear myself, and the company bears another identical amount separately. Payslips usually only list the amount deducted from your own salary. In addition, eligible non-Japanese nationals, after leaving Japan and no longer having an address in Japan, can apply for the 脱退一時金. In principle, the application must be made within 2 years after losing an address in Japan. Whether you can receive it and the amount are judged based on conditions such as pension enrollment, and it is not a refund of all premiums paid in the past.

The errors in the trial calculation hide in a few places. Your pay from April to June affects the standard monthly remuneration grade applied afterward; if there's more overtime pay during this period, it may also raise your social insurance premiums later. Overtime pay affects insurance premium and income tax calculations. Commuting allowances generally also affect social insurance and 雇用保険 calculations, but the portion that meets tax-exemption conditions is usually not subject to income tax. Bonuses have their own deduction method. After age 40, health insurance adds 介護保険 at 1.62%, and after splitting in half you pay about 2,430 yen more per month. Once the number of dependents, housing loan deductions and such come in, the tax amount changes again. When your payslip doesn't add up, check item by item in this order, and the problem will mostly surface.

Quick summary

  • The trial calculation assumes a monthly salary of 300,000 yen, Tokyo, under 40 years old, single with no dependents, take-home pay about 237,050 yen, about 79% of monthly salary
  • The three social insurance items total 44,070 yen, with 厚生年金 at 27,450 yen the largest; the company actually paid the other half as well
  • Health insurance uses 協会けんぽ Tokyo rate 9.85% plus support fund 0.23%; after halving, the individual's share is 15,120 yen
  • 住民稅 is levied based on the previous year's income, about 150,600 yen a year; people in their first year working in Japan will not have this deducted
  • Once you turn 40, there's an additional 介護保險 charge. After the employer split, about 2,430 yen more is deducted per month
