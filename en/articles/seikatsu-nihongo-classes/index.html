Passing Japanese exams is one thing, but where do you learn those phrases that leave you stuck at the service counter?

How to describe symptoms at the doctor, how to ask about produce origins at the market, how to understand the next step at a service counter—there are specific ways to learn practical Japanese, and there are plenty of official resources available.

If you're in a rush, just scroll to the end for the summary. The actionable steps are all there, and you can read the rest of the article when you have more time.

You can answer JLPT questions, but when you get to the hospital reception desk, you can't explain how many days your throat has been sore—this is a gap almost everyone who has lived in Japan for a while has experienced. Exam Japanese focuses on reading and grammar, but daily life requires a completely different set of skills. Whether it's a pharmacy asking about allergies, a supermarket clerk asking when a package of meat expires, or a city office clerk saying you're missing a document, every sentence is short. If you miss one word, you're stuck. It's incredibly frustrating. These types of phrases are usually at the back of textbooks, but you need them every single day. 🏥

The experience of opening a bank account still feels typical when I look back on it. To open an account, you need a phone number, but to get a phone number, they tell you that you need a bank account. You're stuck in a loop. In the end, it was only resolved because the staff member patiently explained things for a long time. Looking back, half the problem was communication. Whether you can understand 70% of what the clerk says, and whether they are willing to rephrase it for you, makes a huge difference. 'Counter Japanese' is all about these moments; the sentences don't need to be perfect, as long as you can get things done.

For a resource you can practice with at home, the Agency for Cultural Affairs and the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) have a comprehensive website called 'Tsunagaru Hirogaru Nihongo de no Kurashi,' commonly known as 'Tsuna-Hiro.' As the MEXT explanation clearly states, this site is for those who have just started living in Japan and are at the beginning of their Japanese learning journey. The content is divided by life scenarios: shopping, visiting a doctor, going to the bank, visiting the city office, taking the train, and taking out the trash. Each scenario includes short videos and sentences you can practice along with. The interface supports 18 languages, including Traditional Chinese, so you don't have to guess based on English translations. Levels range from basic greetings to handling daily tasks. Spending ten minutes on one scenario before bed is more effective than rote memorization from a vocabulary book.

If you want to find a real-life class to speak in, Tokyo has a system of local Japanese language classes. The 'Tokyo Japanese Language Class Site' run by the Tokyo Foundation for Global Welfare allows you to search for classes near your home by ward, city, or town. Each page lists the time, location, and cost, which can range from free to several hundred yen a month—a significant difference. For example, the 'NIKO NIKO NIHONGO' in Chuo City holds classes at a community center near Tsukishima Station on Thursday nights from 7:00 to 8:30, costing 100 yen per session, with eligibility limited to those who live or work in Chuo City. Edogawa City's 'JAPANESE CLUB YU' at the Hirai Community Hall meets Wednesday nights from 7:30 to 9:30 for 500 yen a month. These classes are organized within Tokyo; outside of Tokyo, you'll need to check your local prefecture's international exchange association. Eligibility is almost always tied to where you live or work, and if a class is full, it will be noted directly on the page.

When choosing a class, the scenario is more important than the proficiency level label. If you want to practice visiting a doctor or handling paperwork, choose a local class labeled for 'daily life' or 'everyday conversation.' It doesn't matter if the pace is slow; they actually practice window-counter dialogues in class. For the honorifics and business writing needed for work, local classes won't help much—you'll still need a formal language school or online course for that. Many people do both: go to a community class on weekday nights to practice speaking, and finish the hospital and bank scenarios on 'Tsuna-Hiro' at home on the weekend. Next time you're standing at a counter, you'll at least understand what they're asking.

  • For self-study at home, use the Agency for Cultural Affairs and MEXT's 'Tsunagaru Hirogaru Nihongo de no Kurashi.' It allows you to practice based on life scenarios like shopping, visiting a doctor, banking, and city office visits. The interface is available in Traditional Chinese.
  • To find a real-life class, those living in Tokyo should use the 'Tokyo Japanese Language Class Site' to search by ward, city, or town. Costs range from free to a few hundred yen per month. Eligibility and availability for each class are subject to the announcements on their respective pages.
  • Community Japanese classes are usually tied to where you live or work. Examples from Chuo City and Edogawa City show eligibility restrictions. Once you leave Tokyo, you'll need to check the international exchange association for your specific prefecture or city.
  • For service counter scenarios, choose classes focused on daily life and conversation. Business honorifics and correspondence require different language schools or online courses, as the learning needs are quite different.

From the editor-in-chief

The first time I was asked about allergies at a pharmacy, I was stunned. My brain was filled with long, textbook-style sentences, and I couldn't even get out a short phrase like 'my throat has been sore for three days.' I ended up pointing to my throat and coughing twice; the pharmacist understood and silently flipped the manual to the page with pictures. I've experienced every scenario mentioned in this article. Those few seconds at the counter really make you want to go home and re-watch the hospital lesson from your textbook.
