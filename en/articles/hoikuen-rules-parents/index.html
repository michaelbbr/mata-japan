The nursery school enrollment handbook: the pages Chinese parents most often overlook.

Nurseries generally do not administer medication, and 37.5°C doesn't always mean you have to pick up your child. Every nursery has different rules, so check your own handbook page by page.

For those who don't have time to flip through the handbook page by page, I've summarized the most common pitfalls at the end of this article. When your child enters nursery school and the orientation ends, you'll be handed a thick handbook. The beginning talks about philosophy, but the rest is filled with rules so detailed they'll make your scalp tingle—how many diapers to bring, sewing loops onto towels, no hoods on clothes. The first reaction of most Chinese-speaking parents is, 'Why are Japanese schools so strict?' The second reaction is to shove the handbook into a drawer and only look at it when something goes wrong. 😅

Medication is the most common source of trouble. Many nursery schools have a policy of not administering medicine. The documents provided to parents in Kitamoto City, Saitama Prefecture, are very clear: administering medicine is a medical act. In principle, parents are asked to come to the school to feed it to the child themselves. The school only does so in exceptional cases, and only under two conditions: either the medication is needed for a certain period to prevent or treat an illness and the parent cannot attend due to work, or the child has a chronic condition that requires medication at school. To have the school administer it, you must submit a 'Medication Request Form' and a 'Medication Information Sheet,' and only bring the daily dosage. So, when you visit the doctor, remember to mention that your child is in nursery school and the school generally cannot administer medicine. The Kitamoto City document directly suggests that parents ask the doctor if the prescription can be changed to twice a day—morning and evening at home. The final dosage frequency is still up to the doctor, but asking this question will save you a lot of trouble later.

Then there's the fever threshold. Many parents believe they'll be called to pick up their child the moment they hit 37.5°C, but it's not actually that black and white. The Children and Families Agency's infection control guidelines suggest contacting parents if a child reaches 38°C or higher and shows symptoms like lethargy, coughing that prevents sleep, decreased urination, or loss of appetite. This is a reference for judgment, not a rule that you don't need to call if it's below 38°C. The 37.5°C mark is for morning assessments before leaving home, and it should be considered alongside the child's energy and appetite, as well as whether they've had a fever over 38°C or taken fever reducers in the past 24 hours. If a child is 37.8°C but has no energy or appetite, don't send them; if they are energetic and eating well, the guidelines suggest that it's not always necessary to keep them home. Some nurseries set their own stricter reporting thresholds—calling at 37.5°C and asking for pickup at 38°C—so check your specific handbook for these differences.

There is a reason for those pages about clothing. Many schools ask that you avoid hooded clothes, clothes with drawstrings, or hair clips, and instead use simple hair ties. The reason is that getting caught on playground equipment, being pulled, or falling off and being picked up and eaten by small children are all types of accidents that have actually happened. Japan has JIS safety standards for cords and strings on children's clothing. When schools care about this, it's not because they are being difficult; it's because they are afraid of accidents. Sunscreen is similar; some schools don't store sunscreen, asking parents to apply it before leaving home. The reason is that every child uses a different brand, and some children have allergies, so the school cannot accommodate every individual case.

There are also some minor details that parents from Taiwan might find quite unusual. Some preschools don't even require kids to bring water bottles, as they just drink barley tea prepared by the kitchen when thirsty. Others mandate that children bring a water bottle every single day, filled only with barley tea. Not every school provides daily photos either; the range is quite broad, from paper contact books and paid photo services to schools that send daily updates via an app. Needless to say, nails must be kept short and everything must be labeled; some schools even require you to write your child's name on every single diaper.

At the end of the day, there is no unified national handbook for parents. The state provides the framework for safety and infectious diseases, but the real rules are in each school's own handbook. Take an evening to read through the one in your drawer before your child gets sick or you get rejected—it will be much better than panicking on the phone the day something happens.

Quick summary

  • Nurseries generally do not administer medication. When visiting a doctor, proactively tell them your child is in nursery school and ask if they can arrange for medication to be taken at home. Nursery staff administering medicine is the exception; you must submit a 'Medication Request Form' along with the prescription information, and only bring the daily dose.
  • The rule that you must pick up your child if they have a fever of 37.5°C or higher is standard. The national guideline for contacting the school is 38°C or higher, combined with poor mood or appetite; however, if it's over 37.5°C in the morning, you must also consider the child's overall physical condition.
  • Hoodies, drawstring clothing, and hair clips are often discouraged due to the risk of them getting caught on playground equipment or posing a choking hazard. There is actually a JIS national standard for the length of cords on children's clothing.
  • Water bottles, sunscreen, and daily photo requirements vary by school. It is normal for some schools to require them and others not to; always follow your own school's handbook.

Source materials

  • Kitamoto City, Saitama Prefecture: 'Regarding Medication at Nursery Schools': Administering medication is a medical procedure. In principle, parents should handle it; schools only assist in exceptional cases and require specific documentation (verified November 2026)
  • Children and Families Agency: 'Guidelines for Infectious Disease Control in Nursery Schools,' Appendix 3: Reference criteria for contacting parents and avoiding school attendance when a child has a fever. From the editor-in-chief

The handbook asks parents to write their child's name on every single diaper, which I thought was ridiculous the first time I heard it. But then I thought about it—with twenty-some kids in a class, all the diapers look exactly the same. If you don't write names on them, the teachers would spend all their time just identifying diapers. I'm actually impressed by parents who can keep their handwriting neat until the very last one; mine starts getting messy by the third diaper.
