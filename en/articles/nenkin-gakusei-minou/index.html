Will unpaid pension from my student days be discovered during the permanent residence screening?

The Student Payment Special Exemption and simple non-payment are different pension records. Permanent residence applications usually require proof of payment for the last 2 years, but this doesn't mean earlier records of non-payment won't be considered in the review.

If you're short on time, there's a cheat sheet at the end of this article comparing the student exemption and non-payment. For those who came to Japan for studies, the obligation to join the National Pension starts as soon as you turn 20. Many people only look back at those student-year premiums once they start working and consider applying for permanent residence. Checking your records early to see what those months count as is what determines how you can make up for them now. 😅

The Student Payment Special Exemption and simple non-payment are two completely different record statuses. According to the Japan Pension Service, in principle, everyone living in Japan between the ages of 20 and 60 is obligated to join the National Pension. Students can apply for the Student Payment Special Exemption to defer premiums during their studies and pay them later. Eligibility is based on your personal income from the previous year: the base is 1.28 million yen, plus 380,000 yen per dependent, plus social insurance deductions, etc. Only your personal income is considered; your parents' income does not matter. You must apply every year. Eligible schools include universities, graduate schools, junior colleges, high schools, technical colleges, vocational schools, and other schools with courses lasting at least one year. Retroactive applications can cover a period starting up to 2 years and 1 month before the application date.

The calculation for the special period is handled separately. The period under the student special system counts toward the 10-year qualification period required to receive the Old-Age Basic Pension. However, regarding the actual pension amount, the special period does not count; if you do not pay it back, it is not reflected in the amount. You have 10 years to pay back (tsuino) the approved period. If you pay back from the 3rd fiscal year after the year of approval, interest is added to the premium. Retroactive payment only applies to periods approved for exemption, payment deferral, or the student special payment system.

If you did not pay premiums and did not obtain approval for the 'Special Payment System for Students,' exemptions, or other payment deferrals, you may have a record of non-payment. Simple non-payment does not qualify for retroactive payment (tsuino), and after 2 years from the payment deadline, the right to pay expires. If you neither paid nor filed any paperwork during your student years, you cannot pay it back years after graduation; you must apply as is. Those who filed the paperwork have 10 years to pay back premiums, which can increase the pension amount you receive in the future. Being approved for the student special payment system does not automatically result in a non-payment record just because you haven't paid it back yet.

Regarding the Permanent Residence guidelines revised on October 1, 2026, specifically section 2-4(3) on pensions: it looks at future expected benefits. The calculation method and how to supplement with financial assets were already covered in the Permanent Residence checklist post from November 21, 2026, so I won't go into detail here.

Another key point of the review is Section 2-5(3), 'Payment of Taxes and Public Charges' (公租公課の支払). The guidelines state that public charges include burdens other than taxes, such as public health insurance and public pension premiums. Failure to pay these appropriately will result in a negative evaluation. Even if you have paid off any arrears by the time you apply, if it is determined that you have had past delinquency dispositions or failed to pay as required, it will generally still be viewed negatively. Paying off your balance at the time of application doesn't mean you're in the clear; whether you paid on time is part of the review. The guidelines do not specify how many months of non-payment during your student years will lead to a rejection, nor do they specify how many years back the pension review goes.

The pension proof required for the application covers the most recent 2 years. This includes the 'Notification of Insured Person's Record' issued by the pension office, 'Record of Payment I and II,' the full-record version of the 'Nenkin Teikibin,' printed monthly records from Nenkin Net, or copies of National Pension premium receipts. The postcard-style report sent annually cannot be used as it does not show the full period; the full-period version takes about 2 months to request from the pension office. Be sure to black out personal information like your Basic Pension Number before submitting. Those who were enrolled in the National Pension within the last 2 years must prepare payment proof such as receipts according to the required document list. If you cannot provide certain documents, you may need to submit a statement of reasons or other proof. If you have been enrolled in the National Pension for the last 24 months and can provide complete receipts, some pension record documents can be omitted.

The pension payment certificate required for general permanent residence applications typically covers the most recent 2 years, though some categories require the most recent 1 year. However, depending on the documents submitted, the Immigration Services Agency may still see earlier pension records. Therefore, unpaid records from student years, even if older than 2 years, cannot be assumed to have no impact on the review. Administrative scriveners have varying practical views on the impact of past unpaid records. Some firms take a conservative stance, suggesting that even short-term unpaid periods could affect the review and recommend applying only after accumulating a certain period of regular payments. Other firms believe that unpaid records from student years, dating back many years, may not constitute a sole reason for rejection. The Immigration Services Agency has not published a unified standard for approval based on the number of unpaid months or the time elapsed since late payment. These are both practical views from administrative scriveners; the government has not stated this. Whether the Immigration Services Agency will check pension records older than 2 years is not officially explained. This is included here so everyone is aware that there are two schools of thought, as the government has not yet announced specific criteria for such cases.

Before applying, it is worth checking your record status. Log in to Nenkin Net to confirm your monthly pension enrollment and payment status. 'Student Special Payment,' 'Payment Deferral,' 'Exemption,' and 'Non-payment' are all different records. If you find blank or unclear months, do not assume they are non-payments; check with your local pension office for the reason. Also, pay attention to the type of school; whether a Japanese language school student can apply for the student special payment system depends on whether the school and course qualify. The Japan Pension Service publishes a list of eligible schools, which you can check directly. If your school is not on the list, check with the pension office to see if you qualify for general exemption or deferral. If you have graduated but haven't started your job yet, and your student special payment period has ended but you haven't joined the Employees' Pension, make sure you have handled your National Pension paperwork for that gap.

The new guidelines for permanent residence will generally apply to applications filed on or after April 1, 2027, with some provisions taking effect earlier. However, current permanent residence screenings already verify the fulfillment of obligations such as taxes, pension, and public health insurance; this is not something that only starts with the new guidelines. If you were a student for a few months, you still have time to check your status on Nenkin Net. Take care of any missing payments or questions before you apply.

Quick summary

  • The 'Student Payment Special Exemption' is a deferral, and you can pay the premiums later. Only if you haven't paid and haven't obtained an exemption or approval will a record of non-payment be created. Once the 2-year statute of limitations for unpaid premiums passes, you can no longer pay them.
  • Eligibility depends on your personal income, with a base of 1.28 million yen plus 380,000 yen per dependent. You must apply every year, and you can apply retroactively for up to 2 years and 1 month.
  • For permanent residence applications, you must submit pension records for the past 2 years. The postcard-style 'Nenkin Teikibin' (periodic report) cannot be used; the full-period version takes about 2 months to request.
  • The guidelines clearly state that past failure to pay as required will be viewed negatively, even if you have paid in full by the time of application. However, there is no official stance on whether a few months of non-payment during student years will lead to a rejection.
  • Log in to Nenkin Net to view monthly records. Student special payment, deferral, exemption, and non-payment are labeled differently. Whether a Japanese language school qualifies for the student special payment must be verified against the list published by the Japan Pension Service.

From the editor-in-chief

Pension records won't fix themselves if you just ignore them. Before you apply for permanent residence, log in to 'Nenkin Net' and check your status. If there's a problem, you'll have more time to deal with it.
