What to put in your disaster kit: A checklist for foreigners living in Japan

Prepare water and food according to the amounts recommended by Tokyo officials; copies of ID documents, cash, and multilingual alerts are the things foreign residents most easily overlook.

You can find endless disaster kit checklists online, but if you buy everything on them, your backpack will quickly become too heavy to carry. If you can't carry it, it's useless. What foreign residents need to add is a bit different from our Japanese neighbors—the difference isn't usually in the food, but in documents, cash, and alerts you might not understand. Keep the bag in your entryway so you can grab it the moment the ground starts shaking; that’s more important than stuffing it full.

The official stance in Tokyo provides a clear baseline. The Tokyo Fire Department's list states that you should prepare about 3 liters of drinking water per person per day, with a 3-day supply as a guideline. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government's 'Tokyo Kurashi Bosai' also recommends at least a 3-day supply of food and water. The numbers sound daunting—you wouldn't stuff 9 liters of water into an emergency bag—so it's better to keep that at home and just pack small bottles and ready-to-eat food in your bag. If you already have a sleeping bag at home, it's even easier; you can cross one item off the list. When I first lived in a share house, I used a sleeping bag to sleep on the floor for a week, and I kept it ever since, which made my disaster preparedness list one line shorter.

ID documents are the easiest thing for foreign residents to overlook. Keep a copy of your Residence Card in a waterproof bag, along with a copy of your passport, and save a photo of them on your phone. Opinions vary on whether to include a copy of your My Number card; keep the card itself out of your bag, as a leaked number is a major headache. Just stick to a method you are comfortable with. Copies of your health insurance card and a list of regular medications are worth keeping, as they are useful for shelter registration and doctor visits.

Prepare cash in coins and small bills. The Tokyo Fire Department lists cash as a valuable item. It's not uncommon for electronic payments and ATMs to go down simultaneously during a power outage. Having 1,000-yen bills and coins on hand means you won't have to borrow money to buy water or rice balls during those few hours when convenience stores only accept cash. You don't need an excessive amount; just enough for two or three days is fine. Carrying too much cash can make it hard to sleep soundly.

When an alarm goes off, the Japanese comes at you all at once. Recognizing the shapes of terms like 'Emergency Earthquake Early Warning,' 'Evacuation Instruction,' and 'Tsunami Warning' is enough for emergencies. Install the 'Safety tips' app supervised by the Japan Tourism Agency on your phone; it pushes Emergency Earthquake Alerts and Tsunami Warnings and supports 15 languages, including Traditional Chinese. The Japan Meteorological Agency also has a multilingual disaster prevention page. Check the location of the shelter in your district in advance and save it to your phone—you usually can't look it up when you're in a rush.

The Tokyo Disaster Prevention list suggests many small items for your bag, such as a mobile power bank, portable toilet, headlamp, whistle, and wet wipes. On a night without power, a headlamp is more convenient than a flashlight because your hands are free to hold onto stairs or carry items. These small items do not weigh much in total; keep them in different compartments of your bag so you do not have to reorganize them before heading out.

For safety confirmation, there is one number worth memorizing: 171. The Fire and Disaster Management Agency's disaster prevention learning page introduces the Disaster Emergency Message Dial 171, a voice message service provided by NTT East and NTT West. Each message is within 30 seconds, and when phone calls cannot get through, family members can use the same phone number to hear the message. There are trial usage days during regular months, so find a weekend to practice with your family so your hands will not shake when a real emergency happens.

Quick summary

  • The official guideline from the Tokyo Fire Department and the Tokyo Disaster Prevention book is to prepare 3 liters of water per person per day, with at least a 3-day supply of food and water. Only keep small bottles of water and ready-to-eat food in your emergency bag.
  • Keep copies of your Residence Card, passport, and health insurance card in a waterproof bag, and save a digital copy on your phone. Do not put your original My Number Card in your emergency kit.
  • Keep cash in coins and small bills. Electronic payments may fail during power outages, and the Tokyo Fire Department lists cash under valuables.
  • Install the Safety tips app supervised by the Japan Tourism Agency on your phone; it supports 15 languages including Traditional Chinese. Remember the Disaster Emergency Message Dial 171, and keep each message within 30 seconds.

Source materials

  • Tokyo Fire Department | Prepare Emergency Supplies for When the Time Comes: For drinking water, the guideline is 3 liters per person per day for 3 days. The valuables section lists cash, bank books, and copies of health insurance cards, while the daily necessities section lists flashlights and radios (Verified November 2026).
  • Tokyo Metropolitan Government Disaster Prevention Portal | Tokyo Kurashi Bosai (Stockpiling Page): It is recommended to stock at least 3 days' worth of food and water. The guide introduces the 'rolling stock' method: buying extra of your regular food and replenishing it as you consume it (Verified November 2026).
  • Tokyo Metropolitan Government Disaster Prevention Portal | Tokyo Bosai (Emergency Go-Bag Page): The emergency go-bag checklist includes a power bank, portable toilets, a headlamp, a helmet, a whistle, towels, and one or two 500ml bottles of water (Verified November 2026).

It provides notifications for Emergency Earthquake Early Warnings and Tsunami Warnings issued by the Japan Meteorological Agency. It supports 15 languages and is explicitly designed for foreign residents. (Verified Nov 2026)
  • Fire and Disaster Management Agency, Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications | Disaster Management e-College: Safety Confirmation: Disaster Emergency Message Dial 171 is a voice storage message service provided by NTT East and NTT West. Each message can be up to 30 seconds long, and one phone number can store between 1 and 20 messages (verified in November 2026)
  • Japan Meteorological Agency | Multilingual Disaster Prevention Information Page: An entry portal where the Japan Meteorological Agency publishes weather, earthquake, tsunami, and volcanic information in 15 languages. The language can be switched on the page. (Verified Nov 2026)

From the editor-in-chief

I have a disaster kit at home, too, sitting on top of the entryway cabinet, collecting dust more diligently than I do. During the last power outage, I dragged it down in the dark, unzipped it, and found a pack of long-expired hardtack and two dead batteries—not a single bottle of water. I ended up standing at the counter of a cash-only convenience store, turning my pockets inside out to scrape together a little over 300 yen, just enough to buy a single rice ball.
