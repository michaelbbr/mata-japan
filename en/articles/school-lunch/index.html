Japanese elementary school lunches started with rice balls, and now even the cost has changed

School lunch (kyushoku) is a full set consisting of a main dish, milk, and side dishes. Starting in April 2026, public elementary school lunch fees will be supported by the national and prefectural governments; the part parents need to handle is the paperwork for allergies.

There are more nuances to kyushoku (school lunch) than you might imagine. For busy parents, there is a summary at the end of the article; save it and read it at your leisure.

When children enter Japanese elementary schools, most provide kyushoku (school lunch), so you do not need to prepare a bento (boxed lunch) every day. While arrangements vary by school, this system has a long history. In 1889, the private Chuo Elementary School in Tsuruoka, Yamagata Prefecture, began providing free lunches—rice balls, grilled fish, and pickles—to children from struggling families who could not bring their own. MEXT records this as the starting point of Japan's school lunch program, based on the simple logic that children who are hungry cannot focus on their studies.

You can understand what kyushoku (school lunch) is like today by looking at the definition from MEXT. Most schools provide a 'complete meal' consisting of a staple food, milk, and side dishes, with the staple food alternating between rice and bread. In the 2021 survey, among schools implementing complete meals, the rice meal implementation rate was 100%, with an average of 3.5 times per week. Side dishes change with the seasons, and soup is served hot into bowls, quickly filling the classroom with the scent of miso soup. The overall implementation rate for elementary school kyushoku is 99.0%; almost every child eats something served through the same process at noon.

Costs have changed significantly in the last two years, and this is the area parents need to update the most. According to a 2023 fiscal year survey, the average monthly cost for public elementary school lunches was 4,688 yen. This is not a small amount. Starting in April 2026, a new system co-funded by the national and prefectural governments, officially called 'Fundamental Reduction of the Burden of School Lunch Fees,' will take effect. It applies to public elementary schools, with a subsidy benchmark of 5,200 yen per student per month for full lunches, calculated over 11 months a year. If the lunch fee in your municipality is higher than the benchmark, the difference may still fall on the parents, as practices vary by region. Junior high schools are not included in this round of national subsidies; their fees follow the arrangements of each individual municipality. If you have two children—one in elementary and one in junior high—you need to look at the accounts separately.

🥛 The flow of money is different than before. This subsidy is funded half by the national government and half by the prefecture, and it goes through the municipality to support school lunch costs—it isn't paid directly to parents. Parents don't need to apply for the subsidy, and generally, no paperwork is required. Once the system is in place, many municipalities stop charging parents for school lunches within the standard amount. How the monthly deduction notice changes or how much the amount is reduced varies by location, so just compare it when you receive notices from the school or municipality. The junior high school portion is handled separately as usual. If the amounts don't match up, ask the school or the municipal office—the sooner you ask, the easier it is to resolve.

Allergies are a different matter, and the rules are much stricter than the costs. The MEXT guidelines for school lunch allergy management are very clear. When a child needs the school to accommodate a food allergy, parents must usually submit a School Life Management Guidance Form filled out by a doctor according to school procedures. Verbal or informal written explanations from parents cannot replace official documents; it is best to confirm what documents are needed with the school before enrollment. The principle is complete removal: if a child cannot eat a certain ingredient, that dish will not be provided. The school will also arrange an individual interview to confirm each child's condition before they start receiving meals. If your child has food allergies, organize their medical records before enrollment and check the school's notices to see if the guidance form needs to be resubmitted or updated annually. If you wait until the school year starts to handle this, the kitchen staff will be in a difficult position.

Kyushoku (school lunch) also features a scene rarely seen in Taiwan: the children serve and distribute the lunch themselves. Those on duty put on aprons and hats, bring the soup buckets and trays into the classroom, and take them back after the meal. For children with severe allergies, the guidelines specifically state that they should avoid even tasks that involve touching allergens, such as serving food or cleaning up, and the school will make other arrangements. These details are written in the guidelines; while they may seem trivial, they protect the sense of security of other people's children while they eat.

Quick summary

  • Kyushoku (school lunch) is a complete meal consisting of a main dish, milk, and side dishes. Rice is served an average of 3.5 times a week, and the implementation rate in elementary schools is 99.0%.
  • Average monthly school lunch fee for public elementary schools is 4,688 yen (FY 2023). Starting in April 2026, the national and prefectural governments will provide joint support, with a standard amount of 5,200 yen per month.
  • The subsidy only goes up to public elementary school, the difference above the standard amount may still have to be borne by parents, and junior high school follows each municipality's arrangements
  • For food allergies, you must submit a School Life Management Guidance Form filled out by a doctor. The principle is complete removal, and this must be handled before enrollment.
  • Kyushoku (school lunch) duties are rotated among the children. For tasks that carry a risk of contact with allergens, the school will help the children avoid them.

From the editor-in-chief

When I was a child, what I feared most was being on school lunch duty; the soup bucket was heavy, the corridor was long, and my hands were shaking by the time I reached the classroom door. Watching Japanese elementary school students in white clothes and hats lining up to serve meals, I just want to tell them, don't fill the soup too full, this is advice from a senior.
