How to order at a Japanese nomikai (after-work drinking party)? Order drinks first, and check the situation before ordering refills.

Order drinks first, get some appetizers to line your stomach, ask before ordering refills, and finish with carbs. Save your alcohol tolerance for later.

The tips for a nomikai (after-work drinking party) are written below. If you are in a hurry, scroll directly to the end of the article; the summary has arranged the order for you.

When attending a Japanese workplace nomikai (after-work drinking party) for the first time, many Chinese-speaking residents in Japan think the test is about how much you can drink. Ten minutes after sitting down, you will realize that what is really being watched at the table is whether you know how to order food.

At some Japanese workplaces, the nomikai (after-work drinking party) involves the new employees helping with ordering. If everyone is ordering a la carte, it is usually easier to organize a toast if you ask everyone what they want to drink first. For food, you can start with cold appetizers that come out quickly, then add main dishes based on everyone's appetite and preferences. Towards the end, you can add some carbohydrates for the shime (the closing dish of a meal), such as a bowl of rice or noodles, so everyone leaves feeling satisfied. If you have booked a course meal, the dishes and serving order are usually already arranged, so you do not need to follow this method of ordering dish by dish.

🍺 Besides ordering food, keep an eye on people's glasses. If someone's glass is almost empty, you can ask first if they would like another. Some people drink alcohol and some drink oolong tea, so do not decide the next drink for them. At gatherings where hierarchy matters more, proactively helping to check the order really can save a lot of trouble. In our editorial department, someone was scolded by a senior after the gathering ended for not helping keep an eye on the ordering. It is hard to say whether every company is this particular about it, but at least knowing these habits means you will be less caught off guard next time.

If you have ordered a nomihodai (all-you-can-drink) plan, check the shop's rules first. Some shops use a glass-exchange system where you must finish your current drink before ordering the next. Also, some plans will remind you of the last order about 30 minutes before the end; actual times depend on the shop's regulations. When helping to order, check if everyone wants another drink first so you don't have to scramble at the last minute.

There is another cost that often surprises people the first time they see the bill: the otoshi (a small appetizer charged as a seating fee) that the shop automatically brings to your table is usually not free. The Japan National Tourism Organization explains clearly that the otoshi serves as a seating fee; the contents are decided by the shop, and you cannot exchange them. I was surprised the first time, too. If there is an extra charge on your bill, nine times out of ten, it is this, so do not be too quick to assume the shop made a mistake.

When I was young, I thought a nomikai was about how much you could drink. I later realized that what people remember is the person who kept the rhythm of the table. Next time you are invited, drink beer or oolong tea as you please, but follow this order when ordering food, and you won't be criticized when the party ends.

Quick summary

  • As soon as you sit down, order drinks for the whole table first, and only start ordering food after the toast
  • Cold dishes come first. They're served quickly and are for lining your stomach
  • Meat and main dishes follow the cold appetizers, and the meal ends with carbohydrates (shime, the closing dish of a meal).
  • When you see an empty glass, ask if they want a refill; don't just refill it for them. Leaving a glass empty without anyone tending to it is the easiest way to be remembered.
  • Otoshi (a small appetizer charged as a seating fee) is mostly a seating charge; you cannot change the contents, and if there is an extra item on your bill, it is usually this.

Source materials

  • Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) guide to drinking culture: The 'otoshi' (a small appetizer charged as a seating fee) served upon being seated at an izakaya (Japanese-style pub) serves as a cover charge, and its contents depend on the establishment and cannot be changed.
  • National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan consultation summary for tourists: The otoshi (a small appetizer charged as a seating fee) that you did not order may still be charged at checkout. Think of it as a type of table fee, and the amount depends on the shop.
