How to order at a Japanese workplace 飲み会? Order drinks first, and look at the situation before refills as well

Order drinks for everyone first, cold dishes to line the stomach, refill empty glasses promptly, finish with carbs; how much you can drink actually ranks behind these

The finer points of 飲み会 are all written below; if you are short on time, scroll straight to the end, the quick summary has the order arranged for you.

At your first Japanese workplace 飲み会, many Chinese residents in Japan think the test is how much you can drink. Ten minutes after sitting down you'll realize what people at the table are really watching is whether you know how to order food.

At some Japanese workplace 飲み会, newcomers are asked to help with ordering. If everyone is ordering à la carte, ask the whole table what they want to drink first, which usually makes it easier to arrange the toast. For food, start with cold dishes that come out quickly, then add main dishes according to everyone's appetite and preferences. Toward the end, add some carbs to finish, called 締め in Japanese, a bowl of rice or a serving of noodles, so the whole table leaves feeling full. If you've booked コース料理, the dishes and serving order are usually already arranged, so you don't need to order course by course this way yourself.

🍺 Besides ordering food, keep an eye on people's glasses. If someone's glass is almost empty, you can ask first if they would like another. Some people drink alcohol and some drink oolong tea, so do not decide the next drink for them. At gatherings where hierarchy matters more, proactively helping to check the order really can save a lot of trouble. In our editorial department, someone was scolded by a senior after the gathering ended for not helping keep an eye on the ordering. It is hard to say whether every company is this particular about it, but at least knowing these habits means you will be less caught off guard next time.

If you ordered a 飲み放題 plan, check the shop's rules first. Some shops use a グラス交換制 system, where you can only order the next drink after finishing the previous one. Also, some plans remind you about the last order (ラストオーダー) about 30 minutes before the end; the actual time is subject to the shop's rules. When helping to order, check first whether everyone still wants to drink, so you don't have to scramble at the last minute.

There is another charge that makes many people pause the first time they see the bill: the small dish of お通し automatically served after you sit down usually costs money. The Japan National Tourism Organization's explanation is clear: お通し also serves as a seating charge, its contents are decided by the shop, and you cannot change it yourself. I paused the first time too. If there is an extra charge on the bill, nine times out of ten it is this; do not rush to think the shop miscalculated.

When I was young, I also thought 飲み会 were about how much you could drink; only after competing for a while did I realize that what the whole table usually remembers is the person who kept the pace right. Next time you're invited, whether you drink draft beer or oolong tea is up to you; order following this sequence, and at least you won't be scolded when it's over.

Quick summary

  • As soon as you sit down, order drinks for the whole table first, and only start ordering food after the toast
  • Cold dishes come first. They're served quickly and are for lining your stomach
  • Meat and main dishes follow the cold dishes, and at the end carbs (締め) wrap it up
  • Refill empty glasses around the whole table as you go; glasses left empty and unattended are what people remember most easily
  • お通し is mostly a seating charge, its contents cannot be changed, and if there is an extra charge on your bill, it is most likely this

Source materials

  • Japan National Tourism Organization (JNTO) explanation of drinking culture: the お通し served after you sit down at an izakaya also serves as a seating fee, its contents depend on the shop and cannot be changed
  • Summary by the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan of consumer consultations from visitors to Japan: お通し that you did not actively order may still be charged at checkout; it can be understood as a type of table charge, with the amount depending on the shop

When I was young, I was in charge of ordering at a 飲み会 once; I only ordered what I wanted to eat, three plates of grilled chicken skin arrived, and the senior beside me had had an empty cup for a long time and I hadn't noticed at all. Only when I was scolded at the end did I understand: ordering isn't about the dishes, it's about whether you're watching the whole table.
