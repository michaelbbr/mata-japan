For an apartment with 80,000 yen rent, prepare over 400,000 yen before signing the contract

Break down the security deposit, key money, and agency fees one by one; check what is refundable and what has a cap against the estimate before signing

If you're short on time, just check the items in the summary at the end of this article against your quote. Most people looking for an apartment in Tokyo for the first time get stuck on the same thing. The listing says the rent is 80,000 yen, which seems affordable, but when the agent hands you the quote, the total jumps to over 400,000 yen. You aren't being scammed; the money is just scattered across a list of unfamiliar fees, each with its own quirks. 😅

It becomes clear when you break it down. The numbers are honest. Taking an 80,000 yen monthly rent residential property as an example: security deposit (shikikin) is 1 month and key money (reikin) is 1 month—these two figures are usually listed on the property page. The security deposit is a bond that is returned after deducting repair costs and unpaid rent when you move out. Key money is a gift to the landlord; once paid, it's gone. Even though they appear in the same row of numbers, their fates are completely different.

There is a legal cap on agency fees. The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism states clearly that the total agency fee collected from both the landlord and the tenant cannot exceed 1.1 times the monthly rent. When collected from only one party, the principle is a cap of 0.55 times. Exceeding this ratio requires the consent of that party. So, if the estimate shows an agency fee of 1 month's rent plus tax, it means you have already agreed to this arrangement. It's worth pausing to ask how the fee was calculated before signing.

The guarantee company fee is often overlooked. Those without a Japanese guarantor are usually required to join a guarantee company. The initial fee is often a percentage of the monthly rent, and there may be annual renewal fees later. Check the estimate to see which company it is and how much the annual fee will be. Fire insurance and lock-changing fees aren't huge, but they are easily hidden in the total. It's worth checking the term of the insurance and whether the lock change is for the whole set or just the cylinder.

When you factor in time, the total amount adds up. The rent for the month you move in is prorated, and when you add the full rent for the following month, the rent alone is nearly 1.5 months' worth. With security deposit (shikikin) and key money (reikin) at 1 month each, agency fees at 1.1 months, and a guarantee company fee of about half a month, the total comes to approximately 5.1 months. At a monthly rent of 80,000 yen, that's about 408,000 yen, not including insurance or lock-changing fees. Seeing a range of 4 to 5 months isn't the agent trying to scare you; it's just how this system is designed.

If you want to keep initial costs down, UR Rental Housing is a viable path. The official UR page is straightforward: no key money (reikin), no agency fees, no renewal fees, and no guarantor required. Upon moving in, you only pay the security deposit (shikikin, equal to 2 months' rent), prorated rent, and common service fees. Properties are mostly large-scale housing complexes (danchi), and the locations and layouts differ from private apartments. You trade commute time and floor plans for those months of saved fees—everyone has to do their own math.

Beyond the estimate, you must see the room in person. I once lived in a share house where I signed the contract based only on website photos, only to find the room dim and too small for even a single bed upon arrival. I slept on the floor for a week and didn't know how to turn on the water heater, so I showered in cold water during winter. No matter how carefully you calculate initial costs, if you sign without seeing the room, you'll end up paying for it in other ways. Photos on a listing and the estimate are two different things; verify both before signing.

Quick summary

  • Calculation basis: Monthly rent 80,000 yen, security deposit (shikikin) 1 month, key money (reikin) 1 month, agency fee 1.1 months, guarantee company fee 0.5 months, and rent for about 1.5 months. The total is approximately 5.1 months, which is about 408,000 yen. Insurance and lock replacement fees are extra.
  • The security deposit is settled and returned when you move out, but key money is non-refundable once paid; look at them separately on the estimate
  • The total agency fee is capped at 1.1 times the monthly rent. If charged to only one party, the principle is 0.55 times; exceeding this requires the consent of that party.
  • Check the initial ratio and annual renewal fees for the guarantee company, and double-check the insurance term and lock-changing details before signing.
  • UR rentals do not require key money (reikin), agency fees, lease renewal fees, or a guarantor. Move-in requires a security deposit (shikikin) equivalent to 2 months' rent, plus prorated rent and common service fees for the current month.

From the editor-in-chief

When the estimate was laid out, I, Choko, did the same thing: I ran my finger down the list and stopped at the guarantee company line, wondering what exactly this company was guaranteeing for me. I later realized it guarantees that the landlord is willing to hand over the keys to a mysterious middle-aged guy like me. Fine, I paid the money. The day I got the keys, I went out and bought a cheap seat cushion because after the initial costs, that was all the furniture budget I had left.
