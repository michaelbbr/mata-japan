The sea of lights in front of Tokyo Station, two weeks apart from Marunouchi Naka-dori

If you want to photograph Tokyo Station and the illuminations in the same frame, first confirm which street you're going to.

Living in Tokyo, the easiest winter walk is to detour to Marunouchi after work to see the lights. No reservation, no ticket, just walk out of the station. But every year people get confused: Miyuki-dori, right in front of Tokyo Station, and Marunouchi Naka-dori next to it have completely different lighting dates. The starts differ by two weeks, the ends by almost two months. Go in the wrong week and you can only stare at one road that has not lit up yet, or has already finished.

Two roads: sort out the dates first

First, the conclusion. Marunouchi Naka-dori's 'Marunouchi Illumination 2026' is in its 25th year this year, lighting up on November 12, 2026 and staying lit until February 14, 2027, every day from 16:00 to 23:00, extended to 24:00 throughout December. Miyuki-dori appears later, starting only on November 27, 2026, and ends on December 25, with lighting hours from 16:00 to 24:00. In other words, the only period when both roads are lit at the same time is these 29 days from November 27 to December 25.

On November 12, the day of the lighting ceremony, the start time was different from usual because of the ceremony. It has now returned to normal, with the lights turning on at 16:00 every day.

There is another difference in timing worth remembering: although Gyoko-dori is lit for only 29 days, it is lit until 24:00 every day; Naka-dori normally only goes until 23:00, and only in December is it extended as well. The official announcement also notes that the lighting times for a few sections may differ slightly, and the actual on-site times are the standard.

Different scale, different atmosphere

✨ Naka-dori is the main venue: about 1.2 kilometers of street trees, about 200 trees, about 572,000 champagne-gold LEDs, a long corridor made for walking and photographing slowly. Gyoko-dori is much smaller in scale, about 20 trees and about 60,000 lights, but its location is irreplaceable: it faces the Marunouchi station building of Tokyo Station, and if you want the red-brick station and a sea of lights in the same frame, this is the street. Together the two sides total over 630,000 lights, all champagne gold, not multicolored, and walking through them is surprisingly quiet.

Which dates are best to go

If you want to cover both streets in one go, aim for November 27 to December 25; and Naka-dori also extends its illumination until 24:00 in December, so you don't have to rush after work. The last night on Gyoko-dori is exactly Christmas Day; miss it and you'll really have to wait until next year! If you miss that period, it's okay too: after Gyoko-dori wraps up, Naka-dori will keep shining on its own until February 14, just in time for Valentine's Day. If you don't want to squeeze in with the Christmas crowds, you can also save Naka-dori for after January and take a slow walk on a weekday evening. That's the benefit of living here: you don't have to crowd with everyone during Christmas week, and there are still many evenings to enjoy the lights slowly.

Taking photos: the way to photograph the two routes is different

行幸通 is the fixed-point type: stand on the street and shoot toward Tokyo Station, the red-brick station building in the center and two rows of illuminated trees on both sides, one shot explains it all. 仲通 is the strolling type: the whole 1.2 km is a similar golden corridor, rather than looking for a single best spot, it's better to shoot while walking, including the shops by the road and people leaving work; that's the Marunouchi winter in the eyes of people who live here.

After work, this is how I would plan it

After November 27, when both streets are lit up, this is the route I would take: come out of Tokyo Station's Marunouchi exit, go to Gyoko-dori first to photograph the red-brick station building and the illuminations, then walk slowly toward Naka-dori while thinking about what to have for dinner. The other end of Gyoko-dori connects to the Imperial Palace Outer Garden, so if you have time, you can extend the walk in that direction. In winter the lights come on at 16:00 and it gets dark early, so if you walk over after work, the whole street is already lit. Of course you can make a special trip on the weekend, but stopping by for ten-odd minutes after work on a weekday is really enough.

From the editor-in-chief

I once stood in front of Gyoko-dori staring at a whole row of unlit lights, wondering why it was so dark this year. Only when I walked to neighboring Naka-dori did I realize that was the street that was lit, and I had been looking at the wrong side of the dates all along. In Tokyo in winter, before the romance, check the calendar properly first.
