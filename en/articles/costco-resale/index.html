Even without a membership card, you can buy that small pack of Costco dinner rolls near home

The individual membership at Costco itself costs 5,280 yen per year. Resale shops split bulk packs into smaller portions, giving people who live in the city without a car another way to buy

If you are in a hurry, the key points are all in the quick summary at the end; scroll to the bottom, read it, and you can go shopping.

Living in a small studio in the city, the freezer compartment of the fridge is only that big. Costco dinner rolls smell tempting. After toasting, the butter aroma slowly spreads and the crust gets a little crisp, but the problem is that a whole bag is really too much for one person, and toward the end you start avoiding it. Bagels, muffins, and toilet paper are the same: the items themselves are all good to use, there is just nowhere at home to put them. Wanting to eat it but worrying that buying it will burst the fridge, that small dilemma is probably something everyone living alone in Tokyo has run into.

The barrier at Costco itself is right there. Costco is a membership warehouse store. In Japan, the individual Gold Star membership costs 5,280 yen per year, and the higher-tier Executive costs 10,560 yen, both tax included. Warehouse stores are mostly in the suburbs. Driving there is a weekend outing, and going by train and on foot takes half a day. If you only go a few times a year, you can't offset the annual fee, and you can't finish the bulk packs either. People often stand in front of the entrance, think it over, and just turn around and go home.

Resale shops take a different route. Businesses use a business membership to stock up at warehouse stores, bring the products back to small storefronts in the city, then split the bulk packaging apart: dinner rolls become a few per bag, toilet paper rolls can be bought individually, and frozen foods are also cut into portions that fit a small household. 🧁 Repackaging, transport, and rent all cost money, so unit prices will be somewhat higher than the original store; this part is honest and visible in the listed prices. Buyers do not need a membership; walk in, choose, pay, and carry it away, no different from going to the supermarket on the corner.

You can see in the numbers how many people notice shops like this. A shopping information service under くふうカンパニー surveyed 10,235 people in January 2026: 70.7% were interested in Costco, and 63.7% knew that resale shops exist. At the very least, the survey shows that both Costco and resale shops attract a fair amount of attention. As for whether it's worth signing up for a membership, the annual fee, distance, and product sizes still have to be weighed against your own lifestyle.

An example you can find in Tokyo is STOCKMART in Kichijoji, located on the third floor of Tokyu Department Store Kichijoji. Compiled store information lists its hours as 11:00 to 20:00. What shops like this have in common is that they're close to the station, the storefront is small, and the shelves are lined with small repackaged portions. If you happen to live nearby, you can stop in on your way home from work, and if you find a small-pack item you want, you don't need to make a special trip to a warehouse store. Resale shops open and close quickly, so before you head out, check the shop's social media account to confirm it's still operating to avoid a wasted trip.

There are a few things to keep in mind when buying. Returns and warranties follow each resale shop's own rules; the main brand's return policy does not carry over directly, so for things like appliances, where follow-up repairs matter, ask one extra question before buying. Check the best-before date on small packages carefully, and it is worth bringing an insulated bag for frozen items on the way home; train cars are hot in summer, and butter and similar items will slowly soften in the bag.

Does living in Japan really make it useful? Ask a resale shop this question, and the answer comes down to your living radius. For people who live far away, don't have a car, and only have a small refrigerator at home, paying a little for portioning buys you the ease of not having to stockpile. When you want a dinner roll for dinner, you can buy one right in your neighborhood, the oven heat rises, the room fills with the smell of butter, and dinner for one feels pretty good too.

Quick summary

  • Resale shops do not require a Costco membership; you can walk in and buy. They sell bulk products that businesses bought and split into smaller packages
  • At Costco itself, the individual Gold Star membership is 5,280 yen per year and Executive is 10,560 yen. If you only go a few times a year, it's hard to make the fee worthwhile
  • Resale shops have higher unit prices than the original store; the extra covers repackaging, transport, and rent, in exchange for small portions and being close to home
  • Returns and warranties follow each resale shop's rules; the main brand's policy does not apply directly, so ask clearly before buying appliances
  • Resale shops change quickly; check the shop's social media account for its operating status before heading out

From the editor-in-chief

I went to Costco once, and when I came out, what I'd bought looked bigger than my refrigerator at home. I ate the dinner rolls for two weeks straight, and the last few were eaten with a sense of guilt. Now that small packages are available nearby, both my refrigerator and my conscience feel a little lighter.
