Don't rush to sign that move-out estimate

Cleaning fees, wallpaper, and lock changes—the total can look terrifying when they're all lined up. But if you break it down, many of these items shouldn't actually be paid for by the tenant.

If you're short on time, there's a breakdown of that estimate at the end of the post. When you move out, the management company might hand you an estimate listing cleaning fees, wallpaper, and lock changes that's much higher than expected. 😅

原状回復 (restoration to original state) doesn't mean returning the room to a brand-new condition. The MLIT guidelines state it means repairing damage caused by the tenant's intent, negligence, or use that exceeds normal wear and tear. Traces of living there for several years aren't your responsibility. Article 621 of the Civil Code also excludes normal wear and tear and aging. Wallpaper fading from sunlight, floor dents from furniture, or small holes from thumbtacks happen through normal living, so the landlord generally covers those repair costs. Things like cigarette burns on tatami or mold from neglected condensation are what might be charged to the tenant.

The amount you owe decreases over time. Generally, the cost burden for wallpaper, carpets, and cushion flooring is based on a 6-year depreciation period. If the wallpaper is brand new when you move in, it's worth about half after 3 years, a sixth after 5 years, and just 1 yen after 6 years. This calculation is for the value of the wallpaper itself; residual value isn't the same as a cap on total repair costs. If replacing one wall costs 10,000 yen, you'd pay about 5,000 yen after 3 years or 1,667 yen after 5 years. This is a simplified estimate, and the actual calculation depends on how long the wallpaper has been there—it doesn't necessarily start from your move-in date. Tatami mat repairs are usually charged per damaged mat; depreciation is only considered if you're just replacing the surface layer (畳表).

The scope of repairs depends on the unit of measurement. For wallpaper, it's generally calculated by the square meter of damage, though in practice, an entire wall may be used as the unit. If the replacement area is expanded just to match the color, the tenant generally shouldn't bear the extra cost. Cleaning and lock changes before the next tenant moves in are usually the landlord's responsibility if the tenant cleaned regularly and didn't lose or damage the keys. However, if there's a special clause (特約) in the contract regarding these costs, you need to check if it's valid—don't just take it at face value. Contracts often have a clause stating the tenant (借主) pays for cleaning upon moving out. This isn't automatically valid; its validity depends on whether there's an objective and reasonable justification, whether the tenant was truly aware of the extra burden, and whether they explicitly agreed to it.

For those living in Tokyo, there's another document you can check. The Tokyo Metropolitan Ordinance on the Prevention of Rental Housing Disputes (東京都の賃貸住宅紛争防止条例) went into effect on October 1, 2004. If you live in Tokyo, signed a new contract after that date, and used a real estate agent, the agent is required to provide a written explanation before you sign. This covers the concept of 原状回復 (restoration to original state), repairs during your stay, special clauses, and contact points. If you don't live in Tokyo, this ordinance doesn't apply, and the judgment reverts to the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) guidelines and the Civil Code.

On the day of the move-out inspection, if you have doubts about the repair costs, don't sign any documents agreeing to the charges or the final settlement. Ask for a breakdown: room, wall, area, unit price, years of use, and whether depreciation was deducted. Take it home and compare it with the contract and your photos from both move-in and move-out. When negotiating with the management company, clearly state the points you disagree with in writing or via email. Relying only on verbal communication makes it hard to keep evidence for later.

If you can't reach an agreement, call the Consumer Hotline at 188, which will guide you to the relevant consumer affairs consultation desk. Hours vary by region. In Tokyo, you can also consult the Real Estate Consultation Center of the Tokyo Real Estate Agents Association at 03-3264-8000. If your Japanese isn't fluent, Houterasu (法テラス) offers multi-language services, including Chinese, at 0570-078377 from 9:00 AM to 5:00 PM on weekdays. They can explain the system and tell you which office to contact.

After the lease ends and the property is returned, the landlord is obligated to return the security deposit (敷金). Any unpaid rent or repair costs that the tenant is legally or contractually responsible for are deducted, and the remaining balance is returned. A high total on the estimate doesn't necessarily mean your entire deposit is gone and you owe more; they are part of the same calculation and should be checked item by item. According to PIO-NET statistics from the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan, as of May 31, 2026, there were 14,711 consultations regarding restoration to original condition (原状回復) for rental housing in the 2025 fiscal year. Once you receive the breakdown, compare it with your contract and photos from when you moved in before deciding whether to accept the charges.

Quick summary

  • Landlords usually cover ordinary wear and tear and aging. Fading from sunlight, dents from furniture, and small holes from thumbtacks are not the tenant's (借主) responsibility.
  • Wallpaper lasts about 6 years. Assuming it's brand new when you move in, it's worth about half after 3 years and a sixth after 5. After 6 years, the residual value is 1 yen. Note that residual value is not the same as a cap on total repair costs.
  • In principle, the landlord pays for cleaning and lock changes. If there's a special clause, its validity must be checked; you can't just go by what's written in the contract.
  • If you have doubts about the amount during the move-out inspection, don't sign any documents agreeing to the costs. Ask for a breakdown including the room, specific wall, area, unit price, and years of use, then compare it with your contract and move-in photos.
  • If you can't reach an agreement, call the Consumer Hotline at 188. In Tokyo, you can contact the Takken Association at 03-3264-8000. If your Japanese isn't fluent, call the Houterasu (法テラス) multi-language line at 0570-078377.

From the editor-in-chief

I've received a move-out estimate before. I stared at the wallpaper section for ages, only to realize I hadn't taken any photos when I moved in, so I had no evidence to argue with. Since then, the first thing I do when moving is use my phone to take photos of every corner of the walls before I even start unpacking.
