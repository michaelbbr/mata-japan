Walked two kilometers to get American-style corn dogs from three different stores, but forgot which was which after eating them.

American-style corn dogs at 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, and Lawson all cost around 150 yen. A small comparison in front of the hot food display leads to one honest conclusion.

There's a thin layer of mist on the glass of the hot food warmer next to the checkout counter. The corn dogs lie there one by one, bamboo skewers sticking out, the batter glistening under the lights. You can even smell the savory aroma of the fried food if you get close. After living in Japan for a long time, this scene of hesitating in front of the counter for a few seconds happens almost every week. 🌭

My motivation for comparing these three chains was simple. 7-Eleven, FamilyMart, and LAWSON all sell corn dogs, and I usually just buy one from whichever store I happen to pass by, never having eaten them all on the same day. The prices are surprisingly similar; in the consolidated data from October 2026, 7-Eleven was 150 yen, LAWSON was 148 yen, and FamilyMart was 150 yen. Including the 8% takeout tax, the difference is only 2 yen, so you can't really pick a winner based on price alone.

The process of buying them was more effort than actually eating them. There’s a 7-Eleven and a FamilyMart near my place, but no LAWSON. To get that third corn dog, I walked an extra two kilometers. By the time I reached the store, the heat from the first two had mostly faded, leaving me with nothing but the feel of wooden sticks and paper bags, and a bit of frustration that they were getting cold.

The sight of three lined up together is actually quite cute. The colors of the batter vary slightly; some look a bit puffier, while others have cleaner edges. Biting into one, you first hit the sweetness of the batter, steam rises up, and the sausage inside still has a bit of snap. Squeeze on some ketchup, and the acidity pushes the sweetness back a little. By the last bite, a small piece of batter is still stuck to the skewer, and you just naturally pick it off and eat it.

I was very serious while eating them. Really.

I took turns biting into all three, making mental notes with every mouthful—thinking about the thickness of the batter, the level of sweetness, and the saltiness of the sausage. The more I ate, the more I felt like I was doing something important. In the end, after finishing them all, the only things left in my mind were that they were hot, sweet, and delicious. As for exactly how each one differed, I couldn't say a single word. The notes were all in the feeling, and once the feeling faded, nothing remained.

It's a bit embarrassing to admit, but after walking two kilometers and hitting all three stores, the conclusion I brought home was that I forgot. Maybe there really is a difference between them, but it was masked by the steam and the ketchup. Perhaps you'd need to eat them slowly on the same day at the same temperature to tell them apart. Standing on the side of the road eating while walking, with half my mind on the traffic lights and the other half on whether the paper bag would break, the memory of the taste naturally didn't stick.

I've already thought of how to do it next time. I'll buy all three, put them on the same plate, and eat them in sequence with a sip of water in between, switching up the order. I'll note down the differences on my phone right then and there. There's no need to rush these little things; the hot food display is there every day, and the Lawson is still just two kilometers away. If I walk slowly and let the sweetness of the batter linger in my mouth a little longer, I'll probably remember the comparison better next time.

From the editor-in-chief

I am in no position to laugh at anyone about memory. Last time I went to the supermarket, I stood in front of the shelf for five minutes, unable to remember if we were out of soy sauce or mirin. I ended up buying both, only to go home and open the cupboard to find I already had one of each. Being able to remember to compare corn dogs after walking two kilometers is impressive; I'm the type who forgets the purpose of leaving the house halfway there. Maybe I'm the one who needs the 'sit down and eat slowly' strategy more than anyone.
