Eating dinner alone in Tokyo, restaurants have long since saved you a seat

Ticket machines, partitioned counters, one grill per person: Tokyo shops have designed eating alone into everyday life

On an evening after work, hungry and with sore feet, you stand at the entrance to a shopping street wanting a bowl of something hot. Before you push open the shop door, a small worry flashes through your mind: would it be strange to take up a whole table alone, would the staff look at you twice? After living in Tokyo for a while, you realize that worry is mostly unnecessary. Restaurants here long ago arranged the seats, the process, and the portions for solo diners, to the point where once you sit down, the only worry left is choosing a flavor. Not awkward at all.

The ticket machine is the first sign of convenience. At ramen shops, beef bowl shops, and standing-only soba shops, the first thing you do upon entering is often to press buttons on a machine, select your meal, pay, and receive a small ticket. The payment is settled when you enter, so you do not need to order at the counter or wait for change at the register after eating. The machine screen has photos and prices, so you just point with your finger. On a busy night, this quiet process lets you relax your shoulders.

Ichiran takes this concept to the extreme. Buy your ticket at the vending machine at the entrance, look for the lit number on the vacant seat board, and walk in. Sit at the 'flavor concentration booth,' which has wooden partitions on the sides and a bamboo blind in front. Your field of vision is limited to the tabletop and the bowl of noodles that will arrive shortly. 🍜 On the table is an order sheet where you can circle seven items: broth richness, oil level, garlic, green onion type, chashu (braised pork), amount of secret sauce, and noodle firmness. Hand your ticket and the sheet under the bamboo blind; the noodles will be served through the same small window. The blind is lowered, steam rises in the small space, the aroma of the soup is rich, and you barely need to speak from start to finish. When you want to order kaedama (noodle refill), place the small metal plate on the sensor area on the table, and the kitchen will know.

Yakiniku Like has a different kind of lively vibe. The seating features individual smokeless grills, and you order using a touch panel at your table. You pour your own water from a dispenser on the table, and checkout is mostly done via self-service kiosks (depending on the store). You control the pace: how much you want to eat, how well-done you want your meat, and which piece goes on the grill first. The meat sizzles on the grate, and the aroma of the fat rises, only to be quickly sucked away, so your clothes don't end up smelling too much. It's a place where you can confidently enjoy yakiniku alone. Yakiniku Like has also offered an individual hotpot option where the grill is replaced with a dedicated pot. If you want to try it, check if your local branch currently offers it before you head out.

For dishes like shabu-shabu and sukiyaki that were originally eaten around a shared large pot, Tokyo also has many counter-style shops where each seat has its own small pot in front of it. You choose the broth flavor yourself, and the vegetable and meat plates are served in single-person portions. The person next to you is also focused on swishing their own meat. You occasionally hear the pot gently simmering, and the air carries the light sweetness of kombu and vegetables. Eating hot pot alone, it's fine to go slowly. If the broth cools, just ask the staff to reheat it. No one rushes you, and no one is waiting for you.

Looking back at these designs, what they take care of are all very small things. Seats a little smaller, the process a little shorter, fewer times you need to speak up, and no need to say out loud what you want to eat. After overtime, push the door in, sit down, hot soup in your stomach, and the whole day's fatigue drifts away with the steam. When you walk out the door, the night wind is cool, your stomach is full, and your mood has settled.

Quick summary

  • The ticket machine handles ordering and payment right at the entrance, reducing the number of times a solo diner needs to speak to the minimum
  • Ichiran's flavor concentration booth has partitions and a bamboo blind. Just circle seven items on the order sheet, and place the kaedama (noodle refill) plate on the sensor area to order more noodles.
  • Yakiniku LIKE gives each person their own smokeless grill, ordering via touch panel, self-service water and self-checkout, so you decide your own pace
  • Yakiniku LIKE once offered a one-person, one-pot option. Actual availability depends on each store's current menu. Shabu-shabu counter shops also mostly serve one pot per person, with portions sized for one person
  • Many restaurants in Tokyo have single seats or a solo dining process, so you don't need to be too nervous the first time you go in alone.

From the editor-in-chief

When I first came to Tokyo, before entering a ramen shop alone, I would first check at the entrance whether it was crowded, afraid I'd stand out by taking up a seat. Only after sitting down did I realize the whole row at the counter was people alone, everyone eating their noodles with their heads down, and no one was looking at me. Later, my noodle-eating speed also sped up along with everyone else's.
