No one talks on the commuter train; the rules are written in everyone's actions

The top concerns in the Association of Japanese Private Railways survey are all minor things like coughing, sitting posture, and volume of conversation.

On the 8:00 AM Yamanote Line, the car is so crowded the doors can barely close, yet it's so quiet you can hear the sound leaking from the headphones of the person next to you. If you live here long enough, you realize this silence is maintained by a set of unwritten rules. The things newcomers trip over appear in the same survey year after year. The Association of Japanese Private Railways has been conducting its survey on station and train etiquette since 2001; the 2025 fiscal year survey included 5,202 online responses, and the results clearly show what passengers care about.

The highest vote was for not covering your mouth when coughing and sneezing, at 34.7%. Second was how people sit in their seats, at 31.9%. The survey also asked in detail which sitting posture is the most unbearable: sitting with legs spread open, stretched out, or crossed ranked first, at 45.7%. Third was noisy conversation, at 30.2%. Blocking the area near the train doors, newly included in the 2025 survey, entered the rankings for the first time and took fourth place at 27.6%, and how people use mobile phones ranked fifth at 21.6%. The top items all look like small actions, but they are enough to trouble people who commute every day.

🚃 The lines on the platform are the start of the whole set of unspoken rules. Boarding positions are marked on the ground, with two or three lines formed; when the doors open, stand on both sides of the door and let the people inside get off first, then board in order. Someone in a hurry who cuts into the middle of the line will be reminded by the stares of the whole line. Once on the train, move inside; do not stop at the doorway looking at your phone. That small area by the door is where passenger flow is densest in the whole carriage, and if you block it, people behind cannot get in and the train is delayed as a result.

There is also consensus on backpack position. Railway company manners posters have been saying the same thing for years: take the backpack off your back, hold it in front of your chest or in your hand. In rush hour, the space a backpack takes up is about half a person's worth, and when you turn it can sweep the face of the person next to you. It's the same when seated: put the bag on your lap or by your feet, don't take up the neighboring empty seat. It's even more obvious on days you buy groceries on the way home from work, plastic bags and backpack squeezed into the carriage together, you have to nod to the person next to you just to turn around; if you gather your things in a bit, the whole carriage feels more relaxed.

The rules are in two layers. In general seats it's silent mode, no calls; if there's something urgent, residents mostly reply by message saying they're on the train and will call back after getting off. JR East's explanation is very clear: from October 2015, 37 railway operators in Kanto, Tohoku, and Koshinetsu unified their stance: near priority seats, when the carriage is crowded please turn your phone off, elsewhere set it to silent and don't talk on the phone. You can sit in a priority seat if it's empty. If you see a pregnant person, someone holding a child, someone with limited mobility, or someone wearing a maternity badge or a Help Mark (red background with a white cross and heart, indicating they may need help from those around them), you can proactively offer your seat or ask if they need a seat, without waiting for them to ask.

These rules are not hard to learn; what is hard is remembering them when you are busy. Forgetting to take your bag down, or answering the phone without thinking when it rings, both happen in the most tiring minutes of the commute. People nearby usually do not say anything; they just quietly shift their body a little to the other side. That small distance they move away is this unspoken understanding reminding you.

Quick summary

  • Private Railway Association 2025 fiscal year survey (online survey, 5,202 respondents): what passengers care about most is people coughing or sneezing without covering their mouths (34.7%), sitting posture (31.9%), loud talking (30.2%), and blocking the area near the train doors (27.6%).
  • Line up on the platform following the floor markings, let everyone getting off exit completely after the doors open before boarding, and move toward the inside of the car once you're on. Don't stop at the doorway
  • Take your backpack off and hold it in front of your chest or in your hand, and don't let a bag on a seat take up the neighboring empty seat
  • Set your phone to silent, do not talk on the phone inside the train car, and turn it off near priority seats when it is crowded
  • On priority seats, if you see someone in need board, stand up and offer your seat without waiting for them to ask
