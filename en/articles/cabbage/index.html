Japanese cabbage does not taste right stir-fried, it is originally a vegetable to be shredded and eaten raw

Spring vegetables have a lot of moisture and are suitable for eating raw; if you want to stir-fry, choose the firm winter kind

There's a bit of text. If you just want to know how to buy and how to stir-fry, scroll to the bottom and read the quick summary.

When you buy cabbage at a supermarket in Japan and stir-fry it at home, it comes out watery, with less wok aroma and sweetness, and many people's first reaction is to doubt their own cooking. I used to doubt myself that way too. Later, after buying it a few times and stir-frying it a few times, I realized the problem was the vegetable itself.

The stir-fry style Taiwanese are used to requires thick, durable leaves that get sweeter the more you cook them. But the type I bought was different; it's best when shredded and eaten raw—like the mountain of cabbage next to a tonkatsu—crisp and hydrating, perfect for balancing a greasy main dish. If you stir-fry that kind of cabbage over high heat, the moisture escapes immediately, and no matter how good your cooking skills are, the texture is ruined. I realized later that the supermarket's year-round staple is actually the firm 'Kanyu' (winter) type; I just didn't pay attention to the season and bought the spring variety.

🥬 Japanese cabbage is seasonal. Spring cabbage has loose leaves, a soft texture, and high water content, making it ideal for salads and light pickles. Winter-harvested 'kan-tama' (cold-ball) cabbage has dense, heavy leaves that hold their texture well when heated, making it much better for stir-frying and stewing than spring cabbage. If you want to stir-fry a decent plate of Taiwanese-style cabbage in Japan, checking which season the cabbage is from is more useful than changing your pan.

For stir-frying, choose winter cabbage with firm leaves that feels heavy in the hand. Change the cutting method too: cut leaves into large pieces, shake off water before putting them in the pan, and don't keep flipping while stir-frying; let the edges fry until fragrant and browned before turning. If you buy spring cabbage, change how you eat it: shred it to go with pork cutlet, or mix it into a light pickle, it's much tastier.

Next time I want to stir-fry Taiwanese-style cabbage, I will look for the firm leaves of the Winter type first. If I buy haru-kyabetsu (spring cabbage), I will save it for salads or as a side for tonkatsu (breaded pork cutlet).

Quick summary

  • If you want to stir-fry, choose the winter 'kan-tama' (cold-season cabbage) variety: the leaves are firm and feel heavy.
  • Spring cabbage has high water content, so try slicing it thin for a salad or making asazuke (lightly pickled vegetables).
  • Before stir-frying, cut into large pieces and shake off the moisture. Put it in the pan and let it sear until fragrant and browned before flipping
  • Decide whether you're stir-frying or eating it raw tonight, then choose that season's cabbage

Source materials

  • Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries vegetable-related statistics and explanations: 'kyabetsu' (cabbage) is categorized by harvest time into spring, summer-autumn, and winter types; seasonal classification is not the same as variety classification.
  • General classification by cultivation and distribution: The Spring type has soft, moisture-rich leaves, suitable for salads, shredded cabbage, and light pickles; the Kan-dama (Winter) type has thick, firm leaves, suitable for stir-fries, stews, and okonomiyaki (savory pancake). This article's texture comparisons follow this classification.
  • The differences in taste between Taiwan and Japan mentioned in this article are based on the editorial department's life experiences in Japan: Taiwanese cabbage is suitable for stir-frying, while Japanese spring cabbage is better suited for being sliced and eaten raw.

I went to eat tonkatsu and, looking at the mountain of shredded cabbage on the side, I realized while eating: some vegetables aren't poorly stir-fried; they're just the wrong season for the dish. Next time, I'll just slice that cabbage and eat it raw.
