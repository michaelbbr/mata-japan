Eat your way through Tokyo in Yurakucho this weekend; remember to avoid Monday for errands next week.

Plan your long-weekend activities and shopping for the weekend. Since Kinro Kansha no Hi keeps office counters closed next Monday, make sure to arrange your paperwork and Japan Cup tickets in advance.

Check this out before next week: counters will be closed on Monday for the public holiday.

November 23, 2026, is a Monday and also Labor Thanksgiving Day (勤労感謝の日). According to the Cabinet Office's explanation of national holidays, this date is fixed on November 23 to honor labor, celebrate production, and express mutual gratitude among citizens. With Saturday and Sunday preceding it, it makes for a perfect three-day weekend. On public holidays, city halls and ward offices are closed, and bank counters are also shut. Anything you'd normally do at a counter will have to wait, as you'll only find pulled-down shutters that day. Please schedule any procedures for Tuesday, November 24, or later. Experience shows that Tuesday mornings are usually crowded; if you're in a hurry, make sure all your documents are ready before you head out so you don't have to restart the queue just because you're missing a photocopy. Don't expect much from Friday afternoon before a long weekend either, as people rushing to finish things before the break usually crowd the offices then. If it's not urgent, Tuesday afternoon is actually much quieter, with plenty of empty seats in the waiting area.

Check this out before heading out this weekend: Yurakucho has moved all of Tokyo into a single building.

For those looking to get out this weekend, there is an event in Yurakucho that brings the whole of Tokyo into one building. The TOKYO Shukaku-sai 2026 will be held at the Tokyo International Forum on Saturday, November 21, from 10:00 AM to 6:00 PM, and Sunday, November 22, from 10:00 AM to 5:00 PM. Admission is free, and the venue is just a one-minute walk from JR Yurakucho Station. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government and the Tokyo Convention & Visitors Bureau have brought together tourism associations from the 23 wards, Tama, and the islands. This year, 23 groups are exhibiting, featuring food trucks, local specialties, and regional sake. There will also be a footbath experience and hands-on workshops that require a lottery entry. Saturday’s workshop involves sanding nameplates made of high-quality wood, while Sunday’s features making food samples of shaved ice. With different content each day, walking through the event feels like getting a preview of all your future short trip destinations. The local specialties section is particularly useful for those living in Tokyo; items from the islands usually require a boat trip to acquire, but they will all be lined up in Yurakucho this weekend—you could easily knock out half your year-end gift list here. A heads-up for the following weekend: Sunday, November 29, is the 46th Japan Cup at Tokyo Racecourse. No tickets will be sold at the gate. Admission is 1,000 yen per person, available only via online reservation. The lottery application period runs from 6:00 PM on Sunday, November 22, to 1:00 PM on Tuesday, November 24. If you're interested, remember to submit your application after you finish exploring the event this weekend.

This week's "lesson learned" log: following the wrong person on my first trip to the horse racing track.

The star of this segment went to the horse racing track for the first time and had no idea where to go after leaving the station. He scanned the crowd, picked an older man who looked like a regular bettor, and followed him—and it actually worked. Hearing this, our editor-in-chief Choko could only say that while he might lack a sense of direction, his eye for people is spot on. Things got even more interesting once he was inside; without understanding the rules, he just started buying tickets. Clutching his betting slips and getting nervous along with everyone else, he ended up losing 10,000 yen in one session. His wallet thinned out fast. He had expected the track to be dominated by older men, but was surprised to find many young people, with rows of couples and groups of friends who seemed much more experienced than him. Choko doesn't recommend the "follow a stranger" method—it relies too much on luck. Next time you go to Fuchu, just look up at the station signs; taking your time to read them won't cost you 10,000 yen.

From the editor-in-chief

I also looked up at the signs at Fuchu Station and went the wrong way. I followed the crowd out of the station, and halfway there, I realized everyone was holding the same horse racing newspaper. That was the moment I knew I was on the right track.
