When buying electronics in Japan, there's room for points and a little negotiation beyond the sticker price.

Whether you can negotiate depends on the store, but the calculation for point redemption is written on the official website, so follow that to avoid confusion.

On appliance store price tags, the numbers are huge, but the point-back percentage is printed in tiny text. Anyone buying a large appliance for the first time will likely end up standing in front of the sign calculating for ages, just like I did. 🧾

I only heard later that you can haggle for electronics in Japan. No one in my family has ever actually succeeded, but the advice I heard was simple: if another store is selling it cheaper, show the price to the clerk, and you might be able to negotiate. I made a mental note of that. But hearing about it is one thing—actually walking into a store is another. Policies vary wildly; some clerks might pull out a calculator, while others just smile and shake their heads. Just imagining myself standing there asking for a discount makes me nervous.

The points system is actually quite clear, as the figures are all listed on the official website. Taking Yodobashi as an example: if you pay with cash, debit card, or Edy at the store, you generally get 10% Gold Point back; if you use a credit card, it's 8%. However, if you show the official app at checkout, you can get 10% back even with a credit card. 1 point equals 1 yen. The cashback is calculated by taking the tax-included price, subtracting any points already used, and multiplying the remainder by the cashback rate. Some items have different rates, which is what that small print on the tag is for.

When price negotiation works, there are usually a few common factors. Buying several items like a refrigerator and a washing machine together increases the total, making it more negotiable. Keeping screenshots of online or competitor prices on your phone to show the clerk makes it easier to start the conversation. Conversely, some items have no room for negotiation from the start; some Panasonic models have manufacturer-specified prices, so even if the clerk wants to help, they can't offer a discount. Therefore, the conclusion is that while some items and stores are negotiable, it's normal if they aren't. No one can guarantee a specific discount.

You also need to distinguish between points and discounts. Points aren't cash; they are credit that can only be used for your next purchase. The amount leaving your wallet at the register won't change by a single yen, so if you're on a tight budget, calculate these two things separately before paying. Ask about delivery and installation fees as well. Some stores negotiate the product price, while others look at the total including delivery. If the conditions aren't aligned, you'll talk for ages without reaching an agreement. Write the model number, prices from other stores, and your preferred delivery date on a single note and hand it over. It makes the wait less awkward when the staff member turns to ask their manager.

If you really want to negotiate, you can say this in Japanese: '今日決めるので、もう少し安くなりませんか' (I'm deciding today, could you make it a bit cheaper?). Keep your tone soft and show sincerity. If the clerk says it's not possible, just smile and accept it; you still get your points, and as long as you've clarified the warranty and delivery, the trip wasn't a waste. Next time you're standing in front of a price tag, it doesn't hurt to have the competitor's price on your phone, the app's point screen, and this Japanese phrase ready before deciding whether to ask.

Quick summary

  • For Yodobashi in-store shopping, the standard point return is 10% for cash, debit cards, and Edy, and 8% for credit cards. If you show the official app, you can get 10% even with a credit card. 1 point equals 1 yen.
  • The cashback amount is calculated based on the tax-included price minus any points already used. Some items have different cashback rates, so check the small print on the price tag before heading to the register.
  • There is no guarantee for price negotiations, but buying multiple items at once or showing the clerk prices from other stores or online is a common approach.
  • Items subject to manufacturer-set pricing generally cannot be discounted. For example, some Panasonic models are firm on price, and it's perfectly normal if you can't negotiate them down.
  • You can start the conversation in Japanese like this: '今日決めるので、もう少し安くなりませんか' (I'm deciding today, could you give me a better price?). Keep the tone soft and make sure to mention that you are planning to make the purchase today.
