Weekend outings · AYAKA · 2026.11.27

The ginkgo trees at Jingu Gaien are yellow! The lighting event only lasts until this Sunday.

The Jingu Gaien Ginkgo Avenue lights will turn off at 7:30 PM on November 29. 146 ginkgo trees form a yellow tunnel about 300 meters long. It's free to walk through during the day, and if you go in the evening, you can see two different looks in one visit.

Head toward Aoyama-dori from Gaienmae Station and you'll see the whole street has turned yellow. The ginkgo trees at Meiji Jingu Gaien feature 146 trees in four rows stretching about 300 meters. The treetops form a yellow tunnel, and looking in from the entrance, the yellow seems to stretch on forever. 😊

The leaves are changing a bit earlier than expected this year. On November 19, tenki.jp said the area had already entered its peak (見頃), while Weather News reported it was 70% to 80% yellow, with leaves expected to start falling around December 2. Based on the status announced on November 19, the ginkgo trees are now in their prime viewing period. If there are no strong winds or heavy rain in the next few days, you might still have a chance to see both yellow leaves on the trees and fallen leaves on the ground this weekend.

The illumination only lasts until Sunday. Tokyo's illumination runs from November 21 to 29, from around 4:30 PM to 7:30 PM daily. It's located along the metropolitan road from Kita-Aoyama 1-chome to 2-chome in Minato Ward. If you arrive before 4:30 PM, you can see the yellow leaves in daylight and then see them again once the lights come on—enjoying both views! The illumination is expected to end around 7:30 PM, so there's no need to rush over after that.

The way these trees turn yellow so uniformly is due to a special design. They are arranged by height—taller at the Aoyama-dori end and gradually shorter toward the Meiji Memorial Picture Gallery (聖徳記念絵画館). The ground also slopes down slightly, so from the entrance, the yellow leaves seem to stretch all the way to the end of the road. The Picture Gallery is currently under renovation, so if you go this year, you won't get that classic shot with the building framed at the end; the trees themselves are the main attraction. Up close, the yellow at the top of the trees looks like tiny fans, and when the sun shines through, the edges of the leaves glow.

The ground feels soft when covered in fallen leaves, and they flutter down whenever the wind blows. Be careful where you step, as ginkgo nuts have a distinct smell if you crush them. The temperature drops quickly after the lights come on in the evening, so if you plan on taking your time with photos, bring a warm jacket. When I go out in winter, I always buy a can of corn soup from a vending machine to hold and keep my hands warm—it makes it easier to take photos without freezing.

When it's crowded, please step to the side of the road to take photos and leave the middle for people walking. Large crowds are expected for the final light-up weekend, so if you want to avoid the rush, consider coming on a weekday or arriving early. The official light-up announcements I've found so far don't list any food stall (屋台) festivals. If you're hungry, grab a bite in Aoyama or Omotesando before you head over, or maybe bring a hot drink with you.

After seeing the ginkgo trees, you can keep exploring the area. You can walk from Jingu Gaien toward Omotesando and Harajuku to connect your weekend route. When I have nothing to do on weekends, I like to walk from Shinjuku through Meiji Jingu to Harajuku, or through Yoyogi Park to Shibuya. It's a great mix of walking and window shopping—the Meiji Jingu stretch is best on foot.

Get off at Gaienmae or Aoyama-itchome Station and walk about five minutes to the Aoyama-dori entrance. Follow the trees toward the Meiji Memorial Picture Gallery (聖徳記念絵画館), walk to the circle, and then head back—the light hits differently each way. If you go in the evening during the illumination period, you can see both the day and night views at once. It's a public road, so no tickets or reservations are needed. There are traffic restrictions in the area during the lighting period, so taking the train is the easiest way.

From the editor-in-chief

This article suggests going on a weekday and arriving early before the lights come on, which makes a lot of sense. However, I procrastinated until the evening of the 29th. I rushed to the entrance at 7:20 PM; the lights were still on and it was still crowded. Half of my photos were the back of the person's head in front of me, and the other half were other people's phones. I was only halfway through when the lights went out.
